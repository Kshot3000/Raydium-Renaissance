## 1 · Start at the real pools page

Everything real happens at <https://raydium.io/liquidity-pools/> — live pools, fee tiers, TVL and volume. The tools on my hub (<https://kshot3000.github.io/Raydium-Renaissance/>) only model pool maths so the numbers on that page make sense.

## 2 · Learn the three pool types

- **AMM v4 / CPMM (constant product):** the classic x × y = k pool. Simple, always in range, prices move along a curve.
- **CLMM (concentrated liquidity):** liquidity inside a chosen price range. More fee-efficient in range, earns nothing out of range, needs monitoring.
- **CP-Swap:** the newer constant-product standard — no OpenBook market requirement, Token-2022 support.

Official explainer source: <https://docs.raydium.io/>

## 3 · Before depositing anywhere, check

1. **Fee tier** on the pool page — your earnings come from that fee, split by your share of the pool.
2. **TVL and 24h volume** — fees only exist if people trade; my LP fee estimator shows how volume × fee × your share becomes (or doesn't become) income.
3. **Impermanent loss** — run the price moves you fear through the IL calculator first. If a 2× move's IL scares you, the fees need to be very good.
4. **Token risk** — a pool is only as sound as its weaker token. Verify token addresses from official sources, never from a chat message.
5. **Range (CLMM only)** — decide how you'll notice and respond when price leaves your range.
6. **Deposit ratio (constant-product pools)** — AMM v4 / CPMM / CP-Swap pools take both tokens in the pool's existing ratio, so the second token's amount is set by the pool, not by you. Run your planned deposit through my hub's deposit planner first so the matching amount — and your resulting share of the pool — doesn't surprise you.
7. **Trade size vs the pool** — the closer a swap's amount out gets to the pool's whole reserve on that side, the worse its price gets, without limit: a constant-product pool can never actually pay out its full reserve. If you need a specific amount out, run it through my hub's exact-out model first and look at the price impact — if it's in double digits, a smaller trade, a deeper pool, or an aggregator route will almost always beat it.
8. **Your exit, before you enter** — withdrawing pays out both tokens in the pool's ratio *at that moment*, not the mix you deposited: if a price moved, the token that rose makes up less of what you get back (impermanent loss, made concrete). Run your share through my hub's withdrawal planner against the current reserves so the payout — and what's left if you withdraw only part — doesn't surprise you, and check on the pool page whether any withdrawal fee applies, because the planner models none.
9. **Your CLMM split is set by the range, not by you** — in a concentrated-liquidity position, where the current price sits inside your chosen range decides how much of each token you must deposit: near the bottom you deposit mostly the cheaper-side token, below the range you're entirely in one token and earning nothing, and the narrower the range the more liquidity (and fee share while in range) the same deposit buys — and the sooner price walks out of it. Run your range through my hub's CLMM range deposit planner first so the matching token amount and the tick range don't surprise you.
10. **Watch what your CLMM position becomes, not what it was** — a CLMM position converts itself as price moves: as price rises inside your range you steadily end up holding more of token B and less of the token A that rose, and outside the range you're entirely in one token earning nothing. Before you open a position — and when you check on one — run its liquidity (my hub's CLMM range planner reports it) and range through the hub's CLMM position checker at the prices you fear, so the mix you'd actually hold there doesn't surprise you.
11. **Set your slippage tolerance deliberately** — the tolerance is what turns an expected amount out into a minimum you'll actually accept: too tight and ordinary price movement makes your transactions fail, too loose and a bad fill can take the whole band. The bound should be floored, never rounded up — a minimum that rounds up is stricter than the tolerance you chose. Run your expected out through my hub's slippage & minimum-received calculator so the exact bound you're signing for doesn't surprise you.
12. **Know your ticks before you set a CLMM range** — CLMM prices live on discrete ticks, one 0.01% step each, and pools only accept range boundaries on multiples of the pool's tick spacing: the range you pick is silently snapped (lower boundary down, upper boundary up) before it exists on-chain. Run your boundary prices through my hub's tick / price converter with your pool's spacing first, so the snapped range — not the one you typed — is the one you planned for.
13. **Price a CLMM position against just holding** — seeing what a CLMM position *holds* at a moved price (checklist item 10) is only half the picture: value it against simply holding the tokens you started with. A concentrated position converts itself as price moves, so on value alone it never beats holding — the shortfall at a given price is the fees it must have earned just to draw level, and it grows the further price travels from where you entered. Run your position's liquidity, range and entry price through my hub's CLMM position-vs-holding calculator at the prices you fear, so the fee hurdle — not just the token mix — is a number you know before you deposit.
14. **Estimate CLMM fees from active liquidity, not TVL** — a concentrated position earns its share of the *active* liquidity at the current tick, and only while price sits inside its range: a big position in a quiet tick range can earn less than a small one where the trading actually happens. Estimate the total active liquidity honestly (your own liquidity is part of it, so your share can never top 100%), discount by the share of time you realistically stay in range, and run it through my hub's CLMM fee estimator — then compare the daily figure against item 13's fee hurdle before you deposit, not after.
15. **Plan a CLMM deposit from the balances you actually hold** — item 9 starts from a token-A amount you choose, but a wallet holds fixed amounts of both tokens: inside a range, the scarcer side caps the position you can fund and is used in full, while the other side is partly left over — expecting both balances to be consumed exactly is the common surprise. Below your range the position is entirely token A and your token B is left over whole (and the mirror image at or above the top). Run your price, range and both balances through my hub's CLMM wallet-balance deposit planner first, so the fundable liquidity — and the leftover — is a number you know before you deposit.
16. **Put a timescale on a CLMM break-even before you trust it** — items 13 and 14 each give one half of the real question: the fee hurdle a price move creates, and the fee rate a position might earn per day. Neither half alone tells you whether the position plausibly catches holding: a small hurdle at a near-zero rate can take longer than a large hurdle at a strong one, and with a real gap and a zero rate (no volume, a zero fee tier, or price parked outside your range) the honest answer is never. Run your position and fee estimates through my hub's CLMM break-even days calculator at the prices you fear, and treat the day count as a comparison tool — it assumes the rate holds still, which in a live pool it won't.

17. **Understand why a pool's price is rarely wrong for long** — a constant-product pool's spot price is just its reserves' ratio, and whenever that drifts from prices elsewhere, a trade sized to move the pool exactly to the external price becomes profitable — which is why arbitrageurs pull it back into line within blocks. The catch is the fee: a gap smaller than the pool's fee tier isn't a profit at all once the input is grossed up for the fee. Run a pool's reserves and a price you've seen elsewhere through my hub's constant-product arbitrage model — remembering the external price there is your input, not a live feed — so the size of gap that actually matters is a number you know before you assume a pool is mispriced.
18. **Size a trade by an impact cap, not by gut feel** — item 7 warns that a swap's price gets worse as it grows against the pool, but the usable form of that warning is a budget: pick the price impact you'll accept, then let the pool's depth tell you the size. One subtlety catches people out: the usual impact measure includes the pool fee, so even a tiny trade "impacts" by the fee percentage — a cap at or below the fee tier can't be met by any positive trade, and the same cap in a pool ten times deeper allows a trade ten times larger. Run a pool's reserves and your cap through my hub's price-impact trade sizer before you trade, so the largest size that respects your own limit is a number you know, not a surprise in the fill.
19. **Know what your LP tokens are worth — and that the share moves** — in a constant-product pool you don't hold a fixed percentage, you hold a count of LP tokens, and your share is that count divided by the pool's *current* total supply: every new deposit mints more tokens and dilutes the share a fixed count represents, even though the count itself never changes. What the count redeems for is the same share of both reserves, in the pool's current ratio — items 6 and 8's deposit and exit, seen from the token you actually hold. Run a pool's current reserves and supply plus your count through my hub's LP-token share & value calculator whenever you check a position, instead of trusting the share you remember from deposit day.
20. **Entering with one token? Solve the split, don't guess half** — depositing into a constant-product pool from a single token (a zap) means swapping part of it for the other token in the same pool first, and the right swap size is not half your holding: the swap itself moves the pool's ratio, and the deposit that follows must match the *post-swap* ratio. Swap too little and you're short of the second token; swap too much and some of it is left unused — and the fee makes the swap leg slightly less efficient, so the correct split swaps a little more than the zero-fee split. Run a pool's reserves, your single-token holding and its fee through my hub's single-sided zap-in planner before you enter, so the split is solved rather than guessed.
21. **Leaving into one token? Price the consolidation first** — a withdrawal always pays both tokens in the pool's ratio (item 8), so ending up holding only one means swapping the other token back in the same pool — against reserves your own withdrawal just shrank. That swap's fee and price impact are a real exit cost, and it scales with your share: consolidating a 10% exit from a balanced pool costs about 5% of the withdrawn pair's spot value even at a zero fee, and a 50% exit about 25%. And if you own the whole pool, there is no pool left to swap in at all — you simply keep both tokens. Run a pool's reserves, your share, your withdrawal size and its fee through my hub's single-sided zap-out planner before you exit, so the cost of leaving in one token is a number you know, not a surprise in the fill.
22. **Know how much price room your fees have bought** — fees earned are a cushion against impermanent loss, and the cushion has exact edges: the shortfall against holding grows with the square of the price's square-root move, so fees of 2% of your position cover a rise of +44% or a fall of −36%, and fees of 12.5% cover a rise to 2.25× or a fall to a quarter — past either edge, holding would have been better. The downside has a cap most people miss: a fall can never cost more than half your position against holding (the half in the other token is untouched), so once your fees reach 50% of the position no fall, even to zero, can eat them — while a rise has no cap and keeps consuming fees at ever larger multiples. Run your position value and fees earned through my hub's IL tolerance band calculator to see your own edges as numbers, and re-check them as fees accrue.
23. **Choose a CLMM range as a width, and plan on the snapped range** — items 9 and 12 work in raw boundary prices, but a range is usually picked as a width around the current price — and "±25%" is not symmetric in percentages: built the way prices actually move (upper = price × 1.25, lower = price ÷ 1.25), it leaves +25% of room up but only −20% down. Worse, the range you type is not the range you get: pools snap boundaries outward to tick-spacing multiples first (item 12), so the deposit split belongs to the snapped range, not the raw one. Run your price, width, deposit and pool's tick spacing through my hub's CLMM ±% range planner, so the range, the room on each side and the matching token amount are numbers you know before you deposit.
24. **A routed trade pays every pool it touches** — many pairs have no direct pool, so the trade goes A → M → C through an intermediate token, and each hop charges its own fee and takes its own price impact against its own reserves. The combined impact compounds — 1 − (1 − i₁)(1 − i₂) — so it is always worse than either hop alone, and the shallowest pool on the route can dominate the whole cost even when the other pool is deep. A route that looks cheap hop by hop can be expensive end to end. Run both pools' reserves, both fees and your trade size through my hub's two-hop swap model before you trade, so the intermediate amount, the final output and the combined impact are numbers you know, not a surprise in the fill.
25. **Settle the whole position before you call it a win** — a green number on your deposit is not the same as beating holding: if one token doubles, a 50/50 position with no fees is up +41.4% on what you put in, yet still 5.72% behind simply holding the two tokens — the fees are the whole contest. The honest settlement is one subtraction: fees earned minus the break-even hurdle for the move that actually happened. Earn less than the hurdle and liquidity providing lost to doing nothing, however green the position looks; earn more and it genuinely won. Run the price move, your deposit and the fees you actually earned through my hub's net LP return calculator, so the verdict against holding — not just the return on the deposit — is a number you know.
26. **Concentration is leverage on your capital — price it before you narrow** — a CLMM range position carries the same liquidity as a full-range position for a fraction of the capital: for a range built geometrically around the price (upper = price × m, lower = price ÷ m) the saving is exactly 1 / (1 − 1/√m), so the 0.8–1.25 range at price 1 needs 9.47× less capital than spreading the same liquidity over every price. But the multiple cuts both ways: fees are earned only while the price stays inside the range, and the impermanent loss per unit of liquidity is amplified by the same concentration (item 13) — a narrower range is a bigger bet on the price staying put, not a free upgrade. Run your liquidity, range and current price through my hub's CLMM capital-efficiency calculator before you deposit, so the capital you save — and the concentration you accept for it — are numbers you know.
27. **Creating a pool? Fund the depth your trades need, not a round number** — item 18 sizes a trade against an existing pool; a new pool has the opposite problem: pick the trade size you actually expect and the price impact you'll let it suffer, and the reserves are forced — input-side reserve = trade × (1 − impact) × (1 − fee) ÷ (impact − fee), with the other side set by your spot price. Depth scales linearly (double the expected trade, double both reserves), a tighter cap costs real capital, and a cap at or below your fee tier can never be met at any depth, because the fee alone counts toward impact. And depth sized for one trade is not depth forever: repeated trades in one direction walk the price away, and a deep pool of a bad token is still a bad pool. Run your expected trade, cap, spot price and fee tier through my hub's pool depth planner before you fund a pool, so the depth is a consequence of the trading you expect, not a guess.
28. **A price move rewrites your pool's inventory — know the new split before you withdraw** — impermanent loss (item 3) is usually quoted as a percentage, but the mechanism is concrete inventory change: when token A's price moves by a multiple r, arbitrage trades the pool to reserve A ÷ √r and reserve B × √r, so a doubling of a balanced 1,000/1,000 pool leaves it holding ≈707 A and ≈1,414 B — it sold ≈293 of the token that rose and accumulated ≈414 more of token B in exchange. Withdraw after a move and you receive that rebalanced split, not the split you deposited; expecting your original token counts back is the most common LP surprise. No fees are modelled in that split (live arbitrage pays the pool fee and grows the pool slightly). Run a pool's reserves and the price multiple you fear through my hub's post-move reserves calculator, so the inventory you would actually withdraw — and its value against holding — are numbers you know.

That is why aggregators split routes — and why the split is not 50/50 unless the pools are: deeper pools and lower-fee pools should take the larger share, because the optimum is where both legs' marginal rates meet. Splitting is not free in the real world — an extra leg costs extra transaction fees and can fail independently — so the modelled gain has to clear those costs to be worth taking. Report both bottom lines, because they can disagree in sign — a position at the top edge of its range can be up on its entry value while still behind holding, since holding kept all of the token that rose. Whether the band's edges fall inside your range matters too: an edge beyond a range edge means your fees survive the position going fully single-sided on that side. Small shares and low tiers demand startling volumes: covering the 0.8–1.25 upper-edge hurdle in one day at a 10% share of a 0.25% pool takes ≈52,786 B of pool volume a day. 33. **Plan a constant-product position by the volume it needs, too** — item 32's planning form for an ordinary AMM v4 / CPMM / CP-Swap position: pick the price move you fear and the days you'd accept to recover from it, and the pool's required daily volume is forced — the break-even hurdle on your deposit (the fees that must cover the impermanent loss, priced by my hub's break-even fee calculator) divided by the days, divided again by what you actually capture of each traded unit (the fee tier × your share of the pool). Small shares and low tiers demand startling volumes: covering a 2× move's hurdle on a 1,000 deposit in ten days at a 1% share of a 0.25% pool takes ≈343,146 a day of pool volume. And a zero fee tier earns nothing at any volume, which is worth knowing before the deposit, not after. Run your move, deposit, share, fee tier and deadline through my hub's constant-product required-volume planner, and compare the volume it reports against the pool's real trading on the pools page before you call a position viable.
34. **Entering a CLMM range from one token means letting the range set the swap** — a concentrated position's deposit split is not yours to choose and not the swap pool's ratio: at the current price your range demands a fixed amount of token B per token A (at the range's centre, exactly the price's worth — a value-balanced deposit; near the top edge, almost all B). So a single-sided entry swaps the amount that remaining-A × that ratio forces — on a balanced pool at price 1 in a 0.8–1.25 range, holding only A means swapping ≈51.3 of every 100 A before depositing, more than half, and a higher swap fee pushes the split higher still because the swap leg leaks. If the price is outside your range the answer is simpler and worth hearing before you pay for a swap: below the range the position is entirely the token you hold and no swap is needed; above it, everything must be swapped. Run your holding, your range and the pool you'd swap in through my hub's CLMM single-sided zap-in planner, and check the leftover it reports is the dust it should be — a plan that silently absorbs a real remainder is a plan that's wrong somewhere.
35. **Leaving a CLMM range into one token? Price the swap out of the other leg first** — closing a concentrated position pays whatever it holds at the exit price (item 10), and inside the range that is both tokens: ending up holding only one means swapping the other leg away, and that swap's fee plus price impact are a real exit cost — closing the 0.8–1.25 headline position at price 1 and consolidating in a balanced pool costs ≈4.65% of the pair's spot value at a 25 bps tier, less in a deeper pool. Outside the range the answer splits: at or below the lower edge the position is already entirely the token you'd keep, so there is nothing to swap and the cost is zero; at or above the top it is entirely the other token, so everything must be swapped and the cost is the swap's whole price impact. Run your position's liquidity, range and exit price plus the pool you'd swap in through my hub's CLMM single-sided zap-out planner before you close, so the cost of leaving in one token is a number you know, not a surprise in the fill.
36. **Plan a CLMM deposit from the token you actually hold first** — item 9's planner starts from a token-A amount, but a wallet often holds the other side first: if your funds sit in token B (the stablecoin side of the pair, say), the matching token A is set by the same range maths run in reverse — inside the range your B is worth L = amountB ÷ (√price − √lower) of liquidity, and the A that must sit alongside it follows from that L. Near the bottom of the range the B side is the small side, so a modest B deposit can demand a surprisingly large A match; at or above the top the position is entirely B and needs no A at all, while at or below the bottom it is entirely A and a B deposit cannot fund it at all. Run your price, range and token-B amount through my hub's CLMM token-B deposit planner before you deposit, so the match you need is a number you know, not a surprise at the deposit screen.
37. **Re-centre a position the price has drifted, don't just watch it drift** — a CLMM position's token mix is not the mix you deposited: as price moves inside the range it steadily converts into the other token (item 10), and outside the range it sits entirely in one token earning nothing. Re-centring — closing and reopening a range centred on the current price — resets the mix to 50/50 by value at that price, and it only ever takes one swap: the new liquidity is sized so the re-centred position is worth exactly what the old one is worth, so nothing is added or removed, and the swap is the difference between the drifted holdings and the centred target, priced at spot (a real swap also pays its fee and price impact — items 34 and 35 price that side). If the price has left your range entirely, exactly half the position's value swaps into the other token; if your old range already is the centred range at your chosen width, the honest answer is no swap at all. Run your position's liquidity, old range and current price plus the width you'd reopen at through my hub's CLMM re-centre / rebalance planner before you rebalance, so the swap and the new liquidity are numbers you know, not surprises at the confirmation screen.
38. **Size a CLMM exit before you make it — a partial withdrawal keeps the mix, not the ratio you deposited** — leaving a concentrated position is not the reverse of entering it at today's prices: you receive whatever the position holds at the price you exit at (item 10), which after a move is a different mix from your deposit. Withdrawing part of the position doesn't change that mix either — a position's holdings scale exactly with its liquidity, so taking out 50% of the liquidity pays 50% of the current holdings and leaves the other 50% in the same range with the same mix, only smaller; a 100% withdrawal is the full close and pays the position's holdings in full. If the price has left your range entirely, the exit is a single token — the other one isn't coming back at withdrawal, whatever you originally deposited. Fees the position has earned are collected on top of the withdrawal, not inside these amounts, so check them separately on the pool page before you judge an exit. Run your position's liquidity, range, exit price and the percentage you'd withdraw through my hub's CLMM withdrawal planner first, so both what you receive and what stays behind are numbers you know before you sign anything.
40. **Price a routed trade backwards from the amount you need** — item 24 prices a routed trade from the amount you'd pay in, but the trades that matter usually start from the other end: you need an exact amount of a token (to repay, to settle, to fund a deposit) and the pair only exists through an intermediate. Price it backwards: the second pool's exact-out on your target says how much of the intermediate it must receive, and the first pool's exact-out on that amount says what you actually pay — each hop rounding its required input up, so the figure you budget is never a hair short in the model. The combined impact compounds just as it does forwards (on two balanced pools at 25 bps a hop, receiving exactly 100 takes ≈125.67 in, ≈20.4% combined impact), and some targets have no price at all: a pool can never pay out its whole reserve, so a target that would drain either pool on the route is impossible, not expensive. Run both pools' reserves, both fees and your exact target through my hub's two-hop exact-out swap model before you commit funds, so the amount in is a number you know, not a surprise at the confirmation screen.
41. **Ask how long break-even takes, in days, before you deposit** — items 13 and 16 give a constant-product position's two halves separately: the fees it must earn to match holding after a price move, and the fees a day of pool volume actually pays at your share. The decision lives in the quotient. A shortfall that takes three days of fees to cover is a different position from the same shortfall at three hundred days — and the second is usually a holding you should not be in, because the model behind the count assumes volume, fee tier, your share and the price all hold still, and over hundreds of days none of them will. Two edges are honest rather than comforting: no price move means no shortfall and a break-even of zero days, and a real shortfall at zero volume or a zero fee tier means never — not "a long time". Run your feared price move, deposit, the pool's daily volume, fee tier and your share of its TVL through my hub's constant-product break-even days calculator before you deposit, so the wait you are signing up for is a number you know.
42. **Need an exact amount? Split the target, don't just split the payment** — item 29's split runs forwards from an amount in, but an exact-out need (a repayment, a settlement, a deposit leg) runs backwards: the target is divided across two pools for the same pair so the total paid in is smallest, each leg priced by the exact-out maths of item 7's tool. On two identical 1,000/1,000 pools at 25 bps a target of exactly 100 splits about evenly and costs ≈105.53 in, against ≈111.39 from either pool alone — and the deeper or cheaper pool takes the larger share of the target, for the same marginal-rate reason item 29 gives. The case worth knowing before you need it: a target at or above one pool's whole reserve out has no single-pool price at all, but the two pools combined may still cover it — then splitting is not a saving, it is the only route — while a target at or above both reserves combined can never be paid out, because a constant-product pool never pays its whole reserve. Run both pools' reserves, both fees and your exact target through my hub's split-route exact-out swap model before you commit funds, and remember the modelled saving still has to clear the extra leg's network and transaction costs, which the model does not charge.
43. **A CLMM range is a wall, not a well** — in a constant-product pool a bigger trade just gets a worse price, all the way down; in a concentrated range the trade stops: paying token A walks the price down the range's curve, and at the lower edge the range holds no token B left to pay out, so the most a swap can ever take from one range is the token that range actually holds at the current price. Anything past that continues into the next tick range at whatever liquidity lives there — usually thinner, which is why a large CLMM swap's real fill can fall off a cliff the single-range picture doesn't show. 44. **The second range sets the cliff** — item 43's wall has a far side: a swap too big for the active range crosses into the next tick range and keeps walking there, at that range's own liquidity — and ranges away from the current price usually hold less of it. 53. **APR is not APY — compounding is the difference, and it rests on an assumption** — the fee estimators annualise a day's fees by ×365 and call the result, honestly, a naive APR; the settlement tools count fees held outside the position. What neither shows is what happens if the fees go back in and start earning themselves: at n reinvestments a year the balance grows by (1 + APR/n)^(n·t), and the effective APY, (1 + APR/n)^n − 1, sits above the APR whenever both are positive — a 25% APR compounded daily is a ≈28.3916% APY, compounded yearly it is exactly 25%, and no frequency pushes it past the continuous limit e^APR − 1. But the assumption doing the work deserves more attention than the formula: the APR is held constant on a growing balance, and in a live pool it will not be — volume fades, TVL arrives, your share dilutes, a CLMM position drifts out of range — while impermanent loss runs the whole time and each reinvestment costs a transaction and re-enters at the pool's then-current ratio. Treat any APY figure as an upper-bound sketch of a rate that will move, not a yield you were promised. 54. **A flat end price does not mean a free trip — LVR charges for the path** — impermanent loss compares your position with holding at the end price only, so a token that rises 10% and falls straight back shows zero IL and looks harmless. It was not: on the way up, arbitrageurs bought from your pool at yesterday's price; on the way down they sold back into it the same way, and on both legs the difference between what the pool held and what a portfolio rebalancing at the external price would hold — loss-versus-rebalancing — came out of the LP side. For a round trip of multiple m the cost is Rb·(√m − 1)² on the way up and that divided by √m on the way down (≈4.6537 B per trip on a 1,000/1,000 pool for a ±10% excursion, ≈0.2327% of the position — and the reserves afterwards are exactly the starting reserves, so the same trip can charge you again tomorrow). That is why two pools with the same start and end prices can leave their LPs in different places: volatility itself is a cost, separate from where price settles. Two honest qualifications: the model is gross of fees, and in a live pool arbitrageurs pay the swap fee, part of which reaches LPs and offsets some LVR; and real paths are many unequal steps, not identical round trips. Run your pool's reserves, the excursion you fear and a plausible number of round trips through my hub's LVR round-trip calculator, and compare the per-trip cost with the daily fees from the fee estimators — fees that do not cover the path cost are not income, they are a partial refund.
55. **Seeding a pool sets its price — there is no separate price to choose** — every other check here assumes a pool that already exists, with reserves and a price someone else set. Creating one is the one act where the depositor chooses the price, by choosing the amounts: the opening spot is simply token B ÷ token A in the seed, the seed is worth half in each token at that price by construction, and the LP tokens minted are the geometric mean √(A×B) of the two amounts — exactly 200 LP for a 100 A / 400 B seed opening at 4 B per A — so a full holding of the mint redeems for exactly the seed through the LP-token maths, and every later deposit must follow the ratio you set. The trap is quiet: if that ratio is not the price the rest of the market trades at, the pool opens mispriced and arbitrageurs correct it against your deposit in the first trades — seed a token at 1 B per A that the market prices at 1.25 and the ≈20% gap is a gift to whoever trades first, not a rounding error that washes out. Check the ratio against a reference price you trust before creating anything, and remember a live program may also lock or burn a small minimum of the minted liquidity, which no model here invents a number for. 56. **Ending inside the range is not staying inside it** — the fee tools (checks 14, 16 and 32) all ask what share of the time your position will spend in range, and until now that number was a pure guess. A model can at least discipline it: if the log price wanders with zero drift at a constant daily volatility σ, then after d days the log price is normal with standard deviation σ√d, and the probability the price ends the period inside your range is Φ(ln(upper/P) ÷ σ√d) − Φ(ln(lower/P) ÷ σ√d). A log-symmetric range makes the arithmetic visible — 0.8–1.25 around a price of 1 at 5% daily volatility over 20 days ends inside ≈68.17% of the time, with ≈15.92% above and ≈15.92% below, because both edges sit the same log distance away. But read the figure for what it is: the price can leave the range and come back, so the share of time actually spent in range — the number the fee tools consume — is lower than the ending probability whenever the path wanders, and zero drift plus constant volatility are assumptions a trending or vol-clustering market breaks outright. The volatility is also yours to supply; nothing here reads a market. 58. **A single-sided CLMM position outside the price is a limit order, and its fill price is the range's geometric mean** — place a position in a range entirely above the current price and it holds only token A; as the price rises through the range the position converts into token B, and a range entirely below the price mirrors it for selling B into A. That is an order, not a deposit you manage tick by tick — and its terms are set by the range's shape: a full crossing fills at an average of √(lower × upper) B per A whatever the amount (a 1.21–1.44 range fills at exactly 1.32), and a partial fill to a price inside the range averages √(edge × check), so the wider the range the better the average price and the bigger the move a full fill demands. Three honest qualifications: nothing fills unless the price actually crosses — an order the market never reaches is just a position sitting in one token; while the price crosses, the position also earns swap fees, which improve on the conversion price in practice and which the model leaves out rather than inventing; and withdrawing mid-range hands you a two-token mix, not a tidy partial fill at one price. A range straddling the current price is not an order at all — it starts two-sided, earning and drifting like any LP position. 59. **Pegged pairs trade on a blended curve, and the blend cuts both ways** — a pool for tokens that should be worth the same (two stablecoins, a liquid-staking token against its base) does not want tool 1's curve, which charges ≈9.09% impact on a 100-in trade against balanced 1,000/1,000 reserves. Read the other side of that defence before you rely on it: the curve defends par whether or not par is true. If one token depegs, the stableswap pool keeps offering close to 1:1 for it long after a constant-product pool's price would have collapsed, so traders sell the broken token into it and drain the good side — near-par pricing is the design working as specified, applied to a peg that no longer holds. The amplification is also a property of the pool someone else designed, not a dial you set at trade time, so judge a stable pool by what its A does when its reserves are lopsided (a 1,000/500 pool at A = 100 already spots at ≈0.9917 out per in) and by whether you believe the peg, not by the balanced headline. 61. **A weighted pool's impermanent loss is set by the weight, and it is not symmetric** — tool 2's 50/50 formula is one point on a family: a pool that holds a fixed value share w in token A loses r^w ÷ (w·r + (1 − w)) − 1 against holding when A's price moves by a multiple r, which at w = 50% is exactly tool 2's answer (a 2x move: −5.72%, and ≈85.79 of fees needed on a 1,000 deposit). Two honest edges: a gap narrower than the fee tier is a profit for nobody (a 0.1% gap at 25 bps models to ≈−0.10 B at the aligning size), and with zero fee the endpoint is exactly item 62's depeg endpoint at the reciprocal price — the arbitrageur's profit is the LPs' depeg loss with the sign flipped, which is whose money a broken peg pays out. 67. **On a stable curve, an impact cap is not a drain cap** — item 18 sizes a constant-product trade by the price impact you will tolerate, and a stableswap pool needs the same discipline with a very different answer, because the curve defends par: on balanced 1,000/1,000 reserves at a 1% cap and a 25 bps fee tier, A = 100 admits ≈546.3266 in where item 18's product curve admits ≈7.5947 — over seventy times the trade at the same stated impact. Turn the amplification up and the admitted trade grows again (at a 10% cap: ≈214.9800 in at A = 1, ≈1,063.7013 at A = 100, ≈1,110.1387 at A = 5,000). There is no closed form for the cap on this curve; it is found numerically, pricing every candidate with the stableswap swap model itself, so the sized trade's own reported impact is the cap by construction. But read the post-trade spot before you trust the number: that A = 5,000 trade pays out ≈999.1248 of the 1,000 output reserve and leaves the pool pricing at ≈0.0084 against a start of exactly 1 — the 10% "impact" is an average over a fill that hugged par until the reserve was nearly gone, so the average looks calm precisely while the pool is being emptied. An impact cap limits what you overpay on average; on a stable curve it does not limit how much of one side your trade takes, and a pool left one-sided behind your fill is a cost the cap never mentions. The fee floor is item 18's, unchanged: a cap at or below the fee tier admits no trade, because even a dust trade's impact is exactly the fee. 68. **Compare curves by their impact, not their payout** — the swap models each answer for one curve family, and the obvious next step — run the same trade through a constant-product pool, a weighted pool and a stableswap pool holding the same reserves, and crown whichever pays most — contains a trap: each curve sets its own spot price from those reserves. The weighted spot carries the weight, and the stable spot sits near par only while the reserves are balanced, so on balanced 1,000/1,000 reserves at an 80% input weight the weighted leg pays ≈316.3653 out for 100 in — not because the curve is generous, but because its spot is 4 while the product curve's is 1. The biggest payout can simply be the cheapest starting price. The comparison that means something is price impact, measured against each curve's own spot: that is the cost of the curve's shape, with the starting price divided out. And where the spots do coincide, the payout ranking becomes honest again — on balanced reserves at a 50% weight all three spots are 1, the weighted leg is the product curve exactly (90.70243237 out for 100 in at a 25 bps tier), and the stableswap leg's ≈99.6506 out at ≈0.3494% impact against the product curve's ≈9.2976% is the stable curve defending par, not a pricing trick. 70. **Settle a stable position against holding AND against its deposit — on a depeg they are different losses** — item 62 prices what a broken peg costs a stableswap pool against holding, but a settlement has to add the fees actually earned and say which baseline it means, because on a depeg the two baselines split.

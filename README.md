## Pool tools on the hub (all local, no wallet, no signing)

1. **Constant-product swap model** — the x × y = k maths behind AMM v4 / CPMM pools, with fee and price-impact output. Exact scaled-BigInt arithmetic (9 dp).
2. **Impermanent-loss calculator** — LP-vs-hold at any price multiple for a 50/50 constant-product position, with optional deposit value.
3. **LP fee estimator** — your share of a pool's swap fees from its volume, TVL and fee tier, with a naively-annualised APR.
4. **Break-even fee calculator** — how much in fees a 50/50 position must earn to offset impermanent loss at a given price move (hold value − LP value), and how many days that takes at an estimated daily fee rate.
5. **Liquidity deposit planner** — constant-product pools take both tokens in the pool's existing ratio: given the reserves and a token-A deposit, the matching token-B amount and your post-deposit share of the pool. Exact scaled-BigInt arithmetic (9 dp).
6. **Exact-out swap model** — the inverse of tool 1: given the reserves and the amount out you want, the amount in you'd need, grossed up for the fee and rounded up so the input is never short. Targets at or above the whole reserve out are rejected — a constant-product pool can never pay out everything it holds. Exact scaled-BigInt arithmetic (9 dp).
7. **Liquidity withdrawal planner** — the exit side of tool 5: given a pool's current reserves and your share of the pool, what a full or partial withdrawal returns in both tokens (in the pool's current ratio), plus your remaining share and the reserves left behind. Exact scaled-BigInt arithmetic (9 dp), floored like on-chain programs. No withdrawal fee is modelled.
8. **CLMM range deposit planner** — the range-based deposit the constant-product tools keep pointing at: given a current price, a chosen price range and a token-A deposit, the matching token-B amount the range's position maths requires (the split is set by where the price sits in the range), the model's liquidity, and the range's tick indices. Below the range the position is entirely token A; at or above the top it is entirely token B, so a token-A deposit is rejected there. Tick indices are modelled with floating-point logs and are not snapped to a pool's tick spacing; no fees are modelled.
9. **CLMM position checker** — the follow-up to tool 8: given a position's model liquidity (tool 8 reports it), its price range, and a price to check, what the position holds at that price. Inside the range a rising price steadily converts the position into token B — concentrated liquidity's version of impermanent loss; at or outside either edge the position is entirely one token and earns no fees until price returns. No fees earned are included.
10. **Slippage & minimum-received calculator** — given an expected amount out and a slippage tolerance, the minimum received that tolerance implies (and, with an expected amount in, the maximum input) — both bounds floored in exact scaled-BigInt (9 dp), never rounded up: a minimum that rounds up is stricter than the tolerance set, and a maximum that rounds up authorises paying more than intended.
11. **CLMM tick / price converter** — what the tick indices in tools 8 and 9 actually are: each tick is one 0.01% price step (price = 1.0001^tick), a price's tick floors, and prices outside the standard CLMM tick range (−443636…443636) are rejected. With a pool's tick spacing, it also snaps a boundary the way pools require — lower boundaries down to the previous spacing multiple, upper boundaries up to the next (floor division, so negative ticks snap away from zero going down). Tick maths is floating-point, so a price exactly on a boundary can land one tick off.
12. **CLMM position vs holding** — tool 2's impermanent-loss question for a CLMM position: given a position's model liquidity, its range, its entry price and a price to check, the position's value at the check price (in token B) against simply holding the tokens it started with. The position converts itself as price moves, so its value never beats holding — the shortfall is concentrated impermanent loss at that price, and the tool reports it as a % vs holding plus the exact fees (in token B) the position must have earned to break even with holding. The gap is zero at the entry price. No fees earned are included.
13. **CLMM fee estimator** — the other side of tool 12's fee hurdle: a concentrated position's share is of the *active* liquidity at the current tick, not of TVL, and it earns only while price is in range — fees ≈ daily volume × fee tier × (your liquidity ÷ total active liquidity) × time-in-range %, per day and over a chosen period, with an optional naively-annualised APR against a position value. The total active liquidity is an input labelled as your estimate, and a total below your own liquidity is rejected (your L is part of it, so the share can never exceed 100%).
14. **CLMM wallet-balance deposit planner** — the inverse of tool 8: given the token-A and token-B balances you actually hold, a current price and a chosen range, the biggest position those balances can fund — the scarcer side caps the liquidity and is used in full, the other side is partly left over (used and leftover amounts for both tokens, plus the model's liquidity). Below the range the position is entirely token A (the whole token-B balance is leftover); at or above the top it is entirely token B, symmetrically. Inside the range a zero balance on either side funds nothing and is rejected. No fees are modelled.
15. **CLMM break-even days calculator** — tool 4's question for a CLMM position, joining tools 12 and 13: tool 12's fee hurdle at a check price (the fees, in token B, the position must earn to match holding) divided by tool 13's estimated fees per day at the same liquidity gives the days at that rate needed to break even with holding. Both halves are computed by those tools' own functions, so the numbers can never drift from their own forms. At the entry price the gap is zero and the answer is 0 days; with a real gap and a zero fee rate the answer is honestly never, not a large number. The rate is assumed to hold still for the whole period, which in a live pool it will not.
16. **Constant-product arbitrage model** — why a pool's price tracks the wider market: given the pool's reserves and an external price you supply (no live feed — the tool finds no opportunities), the trade that moves the pool's price exactly to that external price: direction, size grossed up for the pool fee, and modelled profit valued in token B at your external price. The reserves at the target price are forced by x × y = k (√(k/Pe), √(k×Pe)). A price gap smaller than the fee honestly comes out unprofitable. No routing, other venues' depth/fees, or transaction costs are modelled.
17. **Price-impact trade sizer** — the inverse of tool 1: given a pool's reserves (in the direction you'd trade) and a maximum price impact you choose, the largest pay-in that stays at that cap and what it returns, solved in closed form (grossIn = reserveIn × (p − fee) / ((1 − p) × (1 − fee))). Tool 1's impact measure includes the fee, so a cap at or below the fee tier honestly admits no positive trade at all; the same cap in a deeper pool allows a proportionally larger trade. Reserves are your inputs, not live pool state.
18. **LP-token share & value calculator** — tools 5 and 7 work in share percentages, but a constant-product LP actually holds a count of LP tokens: given a pool's current reserves, its total LP-token supply and your token count, your share of the pool (count ÷ supply), what redeeming those tokens returns in both tokens (exact scaled-BigInt, floored like on-chain programs — the same amounts tool 7 gives for that share withdrawn in full), and the position's value in token B at the pool's own spot price. The honest catch: a fixed count's share shrinks as new LPs deposit and the supply grows. CLMM positions are range-based NFTs, not fungible LP tokens; no withdrawal fee is modelled.
19. **Single-sided zap-in planner** — tools 5 and 14 assume you already hold both tokens in the right ratio; a wallet holding only token A enters a constant-product pool by zapping: swap part of the A for B in the same pool, then deposit the remaining A with all the B the swap returned. Given the reserves, the A you hold and the pool fee, the planner solves the quadratic the post-swap ratio condition forces (at zero fee it reduces to s = √(Ra·(Ra+X)) − Ra) for the one split that leaves nothing over. The B the swap removes returns as the deposit, so the pool's final B reserve equals the one you started with — an invariant the tests assert — and a larger fee swaps slightly more, because the swap leg is less efficient. One pool, no routing, no price movement between the two steps; CLMM entries are range-based and differ.
20. **Single-sided zap-out planner** — tool 19's exit mirror: tool 7's withdrawal always pays both tokens, but a wallet may want to leave holding only token A. Given the reserves, your share of the pool, how much of your position to withdraw and the pool fee, the planner withdraws (tool 7's own model) and then swaps all the returned token B back into A against the post-withdrawal reserves (tool 1's own model) — both legs computed by those tools' functions, so the numbers can never drift from their own forms. The honest output is the consolidation cost: the fee plus the price impact of swapping into a pool your own withdrawal just made shallower, priced against the withdrawn pair's value at the post-withdrawal spot price — exiting 10% of a balanced pool costs ≈5% of the pair's value at zero fee, exiting 50% costs ≈25%. Withdrawing all of a pool you own entirely leaves no pool to swap in, so that combination is reported as not completable rather than given a made-up fill. One pool, no routing, no price movement between the steps, no withdrawal fee modelled; CLMM exits are range-based and differ.
21. **IL tolerance band** — tool 4 inverted exactly: given a position's value and the fees it has already earned, the price band inside which those fees still cover the shortfall against holding. Because tool 4's shortfall fraction is (√r − 1)²/2, the edges sit at √r = 1 ± √(2f) — symmetric in the square root of the price, so the percentage moves differ (fees of 2% buy +44% / −36%; fees of 12.5% buy 2.25× / 0.25×), and the tests assert tool 4's own fees-needed equals the fees given at both edges. The honest asymmetry: a fall can never cost more than half the position against holding, so fees of 50% or more are reported as impossible for any fall to consume, while a rise has no cap; zero fees collapse the band to the entry price. Fees counted in the deposit's terms outside the pool, no compounding modelled; CLMM positions are range-based and differ.
22. **CLMM ±% range planner** — tools 8 and 14 take a range as two raw prices; this takes the form LPs actually choose: a current price and a width (±%). The range is built multiplicatively symmetric (upper = price × multiple, lower = price ÷ multiple), so the percentage room honestly differs by side — a ±25% width is +25% of room up but only −20% down. With a pool tick spacing, the edges are snapped outward to spacing multiples (tool 11's rule) *before* planning, so the deposit split and liquidity describe the snapped range a real position would cover; ranges beyond the standard CLMM tick range are rejected. The deposit itself is planned by tool 8's own function, and the tests assert exact equality with it, snapped and unsnapped. No fees are modelled.
23. **Two-hop swap model** — tool 1 models one pool, but many pairs have no direct pool, so a real trade routes A → M → C through an intermediate token. This runs tool 1's own function twice: hop 2's input is hop 1's output exactly as tool 1 reports it (floored at 9 dp — what the second pool would actually receive), and the tests assert exact leg equality with tool 1 at multiple inputs. Each hop pays its own pool's fee and takes its own price impact, so the combined impact — against the product of the two spot prices, equivalently 1 − (1 − i₁)(1 − i₂) — is always worse than either hop alone, and a shallow second pool can dominate the whole route. Both pools are constant-product here; no routing search is done — these are the two pools you typed.
24. **Net LP return calculator** — tools 2 and 4 give the halves separately; this settles the position with the fees actually earned: net position = tool 2's LP value + fees earned, verdict = that minus tool 2's hold value — equivalently fees earned minus tool 4's break-even hurdle, and the tests assert exactly that composition so the numbers can never drift from tools 2 and 4. Both bottom lines are reported: % vs holding and % return on the deposit itself — a position can be up on its deposit (+41.42% at a 2× move with no fees) and still behind holding (−5.72%), and only the holding comparison says whether providing liquidity beat doing nothing. At no price move the hurdle is $0, so coverage is honestly reported as nothing-to-cover rather than a percentage. Fees counted in $ terms outside the pool, no compounding modelled; CLMM positions are range-based and differ.
25. **CLMM capital-efficiency calculator** — why concentrated liquidity exists at all: a full-range position (a constant-product pool, in CLMM terms) parks most of its capital at prices the market never visits. This compares the capital two positions need to carry the same model liquidity L at the current price — the ranged position holds tool 9's own amounts for that L; a full-range position with the same L holds L/√P of A and L·√P of B — and reports the ratio of the two values in B. For a geometrically centred range it collapses to 1 / (1 − 1/√m): the 0.8–1.25 range at price 1 (m = 1.25) needs 9.47× less capital, and the tests assert that closed form exactly. The honest half: the multiple is not free money — the ranged position earns fees only while the price stays in range, and its impermanent loss per unit of liquidity is amplified by the same concentration (tool 12 measures it). The price must sit strictly inside the range; at or outside an edge the position is single-sided and out of the market, so no efficiency is quoted for it.
26. **Pool depth planner** — tool 17's question inverted for pool creators: given the trade size you expect, the price-impact cap you want it to respect, the spot price and the fee tier, the reserves both sides of the pool need — reserveIn = amount × (1 − impact) × (1 − fee) ÷ (impact − fee), reserveOut = reserveIn × spot. The tests assert the composition both ways: a pool funded to the planned depth lands exactly on the cap in tool 1's own model, and tool 17 sized against that pool hands the original trade amount back. Depth scales linearly with the trade; a cap at or below the fee tier is honestly reported as impossible at any depth (the fee alone counts toward impact), and the depth is sized for one trade in an untouched pool — a deeper pool is not a safe pool.
27. **Post-move reserves calculator** — tool 2's percentage with the mechanism shown: when the market price of token A moves by a multiple r, arbitrage rebalances a constant-product pool to reserve A ÷ √r and reserve B × √r — the pool sells the token that rose and buys the one that fell, which is exactly why it trails holding. The tool reports the rebalanced reserves, the change in each token, and the pool's value in B at the new price against holding the original reserves; the tests assert its lpVsHold and IL figures equal tool 2's own at eight multiples, and that x × y = k is preserved exactly. No fees are modelled, so k is held constant (live arbitrage pays the pool fee and grows k slightly); the rebalanced reserves are not what you deposited.
28. **Split-route swap planner** — tool 23's parallel counterpart: instead of routing through two pools in series, split one trade across two pools for the same pair. Each leg is smaller and moves its pool's price less, so the combined output beats the best single-pool route (two identical 1,000/1,000 pools split 100 in evenly and return ≈95.24 vs ≈90.91 single). The split is searched with tool 1's own function evaluating every candidate leg, and the tests assert the legs are tool 1 verbatim, the amounts conserve exactly, and the split never does worse than the best single route. Deeper and lower-fee pools earn the larger share; a pool that cannot take a trade at all is routed around entirely. Only two pools are searched and an extra leg's network/transaction costs are not modelled.
29. **CLMM net return calculator** — tool 24's settlement for a concentrated position, built entirely on tool 12's own clmmVsHold values so it can never drift from that tool: net value = the position's value at the check price plus the fees actually earned in token B, and the verdict against holding is exactly fees earned minus tool 12's break-even hurdle (the tests assert that composition at multiple prices). Both bottom lines are reported — % vs holding and % return on the position's entry value — because they can disagree in sign: at the upper edge of the 0.8–1.25 headline range the position is +5.90% on its entry value yet −5.87% vs holding with no fees. At the entry price the hurdle is zero, so fee coverage is honestly reported as nothing-to-cover rather than a percentage. Fees counted in token B outside the position; no compounding or re-centring modelled.
30. **CLMM IL tolerance band** — tool 21's question for a concentrated position: given the fees a CLMM position has actually earned, the price band around entry inside which those fees still cover tool 12's shortfall vs holding. A CLMM shortfall has no closed form (it changes character at the range edges), so the edges are found by geometric bisection directly on tool 12's own clmmVsHold — the tests assert the hurdle at each reported edge equals the fees fed in. Whether each edge sits inside the position's range is reported too. The downside is capped in a way the upside is not: the most a fall can cost vs holding is the token B held at entry, so fees at or above that cap are reported as unconsumable by any fall, not given a made-up edge. The entry price must be inside the range.
31. **CLMM required-volume planner** — tool 15's question inverted for planning: given tool 12's fee hurdle at a check price and a number of days you're willing to take to cover it, how much volume the pool needs per day — the hurdle ÷ days, divided by the fee capture (fee tier × your share of active liquidity × time in range), which is tool 13's own formula run backwards. The tests feed the reported volume straight back into tools 13 and 15 and assert the hurdle and the day count return exactly. At the entry price there is no hurdle, so the required volume is honestly 0 even at a zero fee tier; with a real hurdle but a zero fee tier or 0% time in range, no volume earns a fee, so the answer is reported as not feasible with an infinite required volume, not a made-up figure. The volume is the whole pool's, in token B per day.
32. **Constant-product required-volume planner** — tool 31's question for a constant-product position, joining tools 4 and 3: given the price move you fear, tool 4's break-even hurdle (hold value − LP value on your deposit) divided by the days you'll allow, divided again by what you actually capture of each traded unit (fee tier × your share of the pool, your liquidity ÷ its TVL) — tool 3's own formula run backwards. The tests feed the reported volume straight back into tool 3 and assert it earns the hurdle in exactly the days allowed, and into tool 4 and assert the day count returns. At no price move there is no hurdle, so the required volume is honestly 0 even at a zero fee tier; with a real hurdle but a zero fee tier, no volume earns a fee, so the answer is reported as not feasible with an infinite required volume, not a made-up figure. The volume is the whole pool's, per day, in the deposit's terms.
33. **CLMM single-sided zap-in planner** — tool 19's zap for a concentrated position, joining tools 1 and 14: a wallet holding only token A swaps part of it for B in a constant-product pool it describes, then deposits the rest — but the split is forced by the range, not the pool ratio: at the current price the range requires rho = (√P − √lower) ÷ (1/√P − 1/√upper) of B per A deposited, and the planner solves the quadratic that condition makes of tool 1's swap curve for the one split that funds it. The swap leg is tool 1's own cpSwap on the split floored to 9 dp and the deposit is settled by tool 14's own clmmWalletPlan, and the tests assert both legs are those tools verbatim; because tool 1 floors at 9 dp, a dust of token A (about a billionth on the headline 100-token entry) is honestly reported as leftover. At the range's geometric centre rho equals the price, so the deposit is value-balanced; near the top edge nearly everything is swapped. Below the range no swap is needed (the position is entirely A); at or above the top everything is swapped (entirely B). The swap pool and the CLMM position are modelled separately; no tick-spacing snapping.
34. **CLMM single-sided zap-out planner** — tool 33's exit mirror, joining tools 9 and 1: closing a CLMM position in full pays whatever it holds at the exit price (tool 9's own amounts for its liquidity and range) — both tokens inside the range — so leaving in token A alone means swapping the token-B leg in a constant-product pool you describe, through tool 1's own cpSwap on the B leg floored to 9 dp. The consolidation is priced honestly the way tool 20 prices it: against what the withdrawn pair would be worth in A if the B leg filled at the swap pool's spot price with no fee and no impact (closing the headline 0.8–1.25 position at price 1 in a balanced 1,000/1,000 pool costs ≈4.65% of that spot value at a 25 bps tier). At or below the lower edge the position is entirely token A already — no swap, zero cost; at or above the top it is entirely token B, everything is swapped, and the cost percentage equals the swap's price impact exactly. The tests assert the withdrawal is tool 9 verbatim and the swap is tool 1 verbatim at multiple prices and pools. The swap pool and the CLMM position are modelled separately; no withdrawal fee is modelled.

Honest labels, always: these are **educational models** using numbers you type in — not live quotes, not live pool data, and not financial advice. Real pools live at <https://raydium.io/liquidity-pools/>.

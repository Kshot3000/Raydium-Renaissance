By Kyle Cox ([@kshot9000](https://x.com/kshot9000)) — my builder notes for Raydium on Solana, for the Raydium team ([@raydium-io](https://github.com/raydium-io) · [@Raydium](https://x.com/Raydium)) and the Solana team ([@solana-foundation](https://github.com/solana-foundation) · [@solana](https://x.com/solana)) and anyone arriving at liquidity pools for the first time. ## 1 · Start at the real pools page

Everything real happens at <https://raydium.io/liquidity-pools/> — live pools, fee tiers, TVL and volume. The tools on my hub (<https://kshot3000.github.io/Raydium-Renaissance/>) only model pool maths so the numbers on that page make sense.

## 2 · Learn the three pool types

- **AMM v4 / CPMM (constant product):** the classic x × y = k pool. Simple, always in range, prices move along a curve.
- **CLMM (concentrated liquidity):** liquidity inside a chosen price range. More fee-efficient in range, earns nothing out of range, needs monitoring.
- **CP-Swap:** the newer constant-product standard — no OpenBook market requirement, Token-2022 support.

Official explainer source: <https://docs.raydium.io/>

## 3 · Before depositing anywhere, check

1. **Fee tier** on the pool page — your earnings come from that fee, split by your share of the pool.
2. **TVL and 24h volume** — fees only exist if people trade; my LP fee estimator shows how volume × fee × your share becomes (or doesn't become) income.
3. **Impermanent loss** — run the price moves you fear through the IL calculator first. If a 2× move's IL scares you, the fees need to be very good.
4. **Token risk** — a pool is only as sound as its weaker token. Verify token addresses from official sources, never from a chat message.
5. **Range (CLMM only)** — decide how you'll notice and respond when price leaves your range.
6. **Deposit ratio (constant-product pools)** — AMM v4 / CPMM / CP-Swap pools take both tokens in the pool's existing ratio, so the second token's amount is set by the pool, not by you. Run your planned deposit through my hub's deposit planner first so the matching amount — and your resulting share of the pool — doesn't surprise you.
7. **Trade size vs the pool** — the closer a swap's amount out gets to the pool's whole reserve on that side, the worse its price gets, without limit: a constant-product pool can never actually pay out its full reserve. If you need a specific amount out, run it through my hub's exact-out model first and look at the price impact — if it's in double digits, a smaller trade, a deeper pool, or an aggregator route will almost always beat it.
8. **Your exit, before you enter** — withdrawing pays out both tokens in the pool's ratio *at that moment*, not the mix you deposited: if a price moved, the token that rose makes up less of what you get back (impermanent loss, made concrete). Run your share through my hub's withdrawal planner against the current reserves so the payout — and what's left if you withdraw only part — doesn't surprise you, and check on the pool page whether any withdrawal fee applies, because the planner models none.
9. **Your CLMM split is set by the range, not by you** — in a concentrated-liquidity position, where the current price sits inside your chosen range decides how much of each token you must deposit: near the bottom you deposit mostly the cheaper-side token, below the range you're entirely in one token and earning nothing, and the narrower the range the more liquidity (and fee share while in range) the same deposit buys — and the sooner price walks out of it. Run your range through my hub's CLMM range deposit planner first so the matching token amount and the tick range don't surprise you.
10. **Watch what your CLMM position becomes, not what it was** — a CLMM position converts itself as price moves: as price rises inside your range you steadily end up holding more of token B and less of the token A that rose, and outside the range you're entirely in one token earning nothing. Before you open a position — and when you check on one — run its liquidity (my hub's CLMM range planner reports it) and range through the hub's CLMM position checker at the prices you fear, so the mix you'd actually hold there doesn't surprise you.
11. **Set your slippage tolerance deliberately** — the tolerance is what turns an expected amount out into a minimum you'll actually accept: too tight and ordinary price movement makes your transactions fail, too loose and a bad fill can take the whole band. The bound should be floored, never rounded up — a minimum that rounds up is stricter than the tolerance you chose. Run your expected out through my hub's slippage & minimum-received calculator so the exact bound you're signing for doesn't surprise you.
12. **Know your ticks before you set a CLMM range** — CLMM prices live on discrete ticks, one 0.01% step each, and pools only accept range boundaries on multiples of the pool's tick spacing: the range you pick is silently snapped (lower boundary down, upper boundary up) before it exists on-chain. Run your boundary prices through my hub's tick / price converter with your pool's spacing first, so the snapped range — not the one you typed — is the one you planned for.
13. **Price a CLMM position against just holding** — seeing what a CLMM position *holds* at a moved price (checklist item 10) is only half the picture: value it against simply holding the tokens you started with. A concentrated position converts itself as price moves, so on value alone it never beats holding — the shortfall at a given price is the fees it must have earned just to draw level, and it grows the further price travels from where you entered. Run your position's liquidity, range and entry price through my hub's CLMM position-vs-holding calculator at the prices you fear, so the fee hurdle — not just the token mix — is a number you know before you deposit.
14. **Estimate CLMM fees from active liquidity, not TVL** — a concentrated position earns its share of the *active* liquidity at the current tick, and only while price sits inside its range: a big position in a quiet tick range can earn less than a small one where the trading actually happens. Estimate the total active liquidity honestly (your own liquidity is part of it, so your share can never top 100%), discount by the share of time you realistically stay in range, and run it through my hub's CLMM fee estimator — then compare the daily figure against item 13's fee hurdle before you deposit, not after.
15. **Plan a CLMM deposit from the balances you actually hold** — item 9 starts from a token-A amount you choose, but a wallet holds fixed amounts of both tokens: inside a range, the scarcer side caps the position you can fund and is used in full, while the other side is partly left over — expecting both balances to be consumed exactly is the common surprise. Below your range the position is entirely token A and your token B is left over whole (and the mirror image at or above the top). Run your price, range and both balances through my hub's CLMM wallet-balance deposit planner first, so the fundable liquidity — and the leftover — is a number you know before you deposit.
16. **Put a timescale on a CLMM break-even before you trust it** — items 13 and 14 each give one half of the real question: the fee hurdle a price move creates, and the fee rate a position might earn per day. Neither half alone tells you whether the position plausibly catches holding: a small hurdle at a near-zero rate can take longer than a large hurdle at a strong one, and with a real gap and a zero rate (no volume, a zero fee tier, or price parked outside your range) the honest answer is never. Run your position and fee estimates through my hub's CLMM break-even days calculator at the prices you fear, and treat the day count as a comparison tool — it assumes the rate holds still, which in a live pool it won't.

17. **Understand why a pool's price is rarely wrong for long** — a constant-product pool's spot price is just its reserves' ratio, and whenever that drifts from prices elsewhere, a trade sized to move the pool exactly to the external price becomes profitable — which is why arbitrageurs pull it back into line within blocks. The catch is the fee: a gap smaller than the pool's fee tier isn't a profit at all once the input is grossed up for the fee. Run a pool's reserves and a price you've seen elsewhere through my hub's constant-product arbitrage model — remembering the external price there is your input, not a live feed — so the size of gap that actually matters is a number you know before you assume a pool is mispriced.
18. **Size a trade by an impact cap, not by gut feel** — item 7 warns that a swap's price gets worse as it grows against the pool, but the usable form of that warning is a budget: pick the price impact you'll accept, then let the pool's depth tell you the size. One subtlety catches people out: the usual impact measure includes the pool fee, so even a tiny trade "impacts" by the fee percentage — a cap at or below the fee tier can't be met by any positive trade, and the same cap in a pool ten times deeper allows a trade ten times larger. Run a pool's reserves and your cap through my hub's price-impact trade sizer before you trade, so the largest size that respects your own limit is a number you know, not a surprise in the fill.
19. **Know what your LP tokens are worth — and that the share moves** — in a constant-product pool you don't hold a fixed percentage, you hold a count of LP tokens, and your share is that count divided by the pool's *current* total supply: every new deposit mints more tokens and dilutes the share a fixed count represents, even though the count itself never changes. What the count redeems for is the same share of both reserves, in the pool's current ratio — items 6 and 8's deposit and exit, seen from the token you actually hold. Run a pool's current reserves and supply plus your count through my hub's LP-token share & value calculator whenever you check a position, instead of trusting the share you remember from deposit day.
20. **Entering with one token? Solve the split, don't guess half** — depositing into a constant-product pool from a single token (a zap) means swapping part of it for the other token in the same pool first, and the right swap size is not half your holding: the swap itself moves the pool's ratio, and the deposit that follows must match the *post-swap* ratio. Swap too little and you're short of the second token; swap too much and some of it is left unused — and the fee makes the swap leg slightly less efficient, so the correct split swaps a little more than the zero-fee split. Run a pool's reserves, your single-token holding and its fee through my hub's single-sided zap-in planner before you enter, so the split is solved rather than guessed.
21. **Leaving into one token? Price the consolidation first** — a withdrawal always pays both tokens in the pool's ratio (item 8), so ending up holding only one means swapping the other token back in the same pool — against reserves your own withdrawal just shrank. That swap's fee and price impact are a real exit cost, and it scales with your share: consolidating a 10% exit from a balanced pool costs about 5% of the withdrawn pair's spot value even at a zero fee, and a 50% exit about 25%. And if you own the whole pool, there is no pool left to swap in at all — you simply keep both tokens. Run a pool's reserves, your share, your withdrawal size and its fee through my hub's single-sided zap-out planner before you exit, so the cost of leaving in one token is a number you know, not a surprise in the fill.
22. **Know how much price room your fees have bought** — fees earned are a cushion against impermanent loss, and the cushion has exact edges: the shortfall against holding grows with the square of the price's square-root move, so fees of 2% of your position cover a rise of +44% or a fall of −36%, and fees of 12.5% cover a rise to 2.25× or a fall to a quarter — past either edge, holding would have been better. The downside has a cap most people miss: a fall can never cost more than half your position against holding (the half in the other token is untouched), so once your fees reach 50% of the position no fall, even to zero, can eat them — while a rise has no cap and keeps consuming fees at ever larger multiples. Run your position value and fees earned through my hub's IL tolerance band calculator to see your own edges as numbers, and re-check them as fees accrue.
23. **Choose a CLMM range as a width, and plan on the snapped range** — items 9 and 12 work in raw boundary prices, but a range is usually picked as a width around the current price — and "±25%" is not symmetric in percentages: built the way prices actually move (upper = price × 1.25, lower = price ÷ 1.25), it leaves +25% of room up but only −20% down. Worse, the range you type is not the range you get: pools snap boundaries outward to tick-spacing multiples first (item 12), so the deposit split belongs to the snapped range, not the raw one. Run your price, width, deposit and pool's tick spacing through my hub's CLMM ±% range planner, so the range, the room on each side and the matching token amount are numbers you know before you deposit.
24. **A routed trade pays every pool it touches** — many pairs have no direct pool, so the trade goes A → M → C through an intermediate token, and each hop charges its own fee and takes its own price impact against its own reserves. The combined impact compounds — 1 − (1 − i₁)(1 − i₂) — so it is always worse than either hop alone, and the shallowest pool on the route can dominate the whole cost even when the other pool is deep. A route that looks cheap hop by hop can be expensive end to end. Run both pools' reserves, both fees and your trade size through my hub's two-hop swap model before you trade, so the intermediate amount, the final output and the combined impact are numbers you know, not a surprise in the fill.
25. **Settle the whole position before you call it a win** — a green number on your deposit is not the same as beating holding: if one token doubles, a 50/50 position with no fees is up +41.4% on what you put in, yet still 5.72% behind simply holding the two tokens — the fees are the whole contest. The honest settlement is one subtraction: fees earned minus the break-even hurdle for the move that actually happened. Earn less than the hurdle and liquidity providing lost to doing nothing, however green the position looks; earn more and it genuinely won. Run the price move, your deposit and the fees you actually earned through my hub's net LP return calculator, so the verdict against holding — not just the return on the deposit — is a number you know.
26. **Concentration is leverage on your capital — price it before you narrow** — a CLMM range position carries the same liquidity as a full-range position for a fraction of the capital: for a range built geometrically around the price (upper = price × m, lower = price ÷ m) the saving is exactly 1 / (1 − 1/√m), so the 0.8–1.25 range at price 1 needs 9.47× less capital than spreading the same liquidity over every price. But the multiple cuts both ways: fees are earned only while the price stays inside the range, and the impermanent loss per unit of liquidity is amplified by the same concentration (item 13) — a narrower range is a bigger bet on the price staying put, not a free upgrade. Run your liquidity, range and current price through my hub's CLMM capital-efficiency calculator before you deposit, so the capital you save — and the concentration you accept for it — are numbers you know.
27. **Creating a pool? Fund the depth your trades need, not a round number** — item 18 sizes a trade against an existing pool; a new pool has the opposite problem: pick the trade size you actually expect and the price impact you'll let it suffer, and the reserves are forced — input-side reserve = trade × (1 − impact) × (1 − fee) ÷ (impact − fee), with the other side set by your spot price. Depth scales linearly (double the expected trade, double both reserves), a tighter cap costs real capital, and a cap at or below your fee tier can never be met at any depth, because the fee alone counts toward impact. And depth sized for one trade is not depth forever: repeated trades in one direction walk the price away, and a deep pool of a bad token is still a bad pool. Run your expected trade, cap, spot price and fee tier through my hub's pool depth planner before you fund a pool, so the depth is a consequence of the trading you expect, not a guess.
28. **A price move rewrites your pool's inventory — know the new split before you withdraw** — impermanent loss (item 3) is usually quoted as a percentage, but the mechanism is concrete inventory change: when token A's price moves by a multiple r, arbitrage trades the pool to reserve A ÷ √r and reserve B × √r, so a doubling of a balanced 1,000/1,000 pool leaves it holding ≈707 A and ≈1,414 B — it sold ≈293 of the token that rose and accumulated ≈414 more of token B in exchange. Withdraw after a move and you receive that rebalanced split, not the split you deposited; expecting your original token counts back is the most common LP surprise. No fees are modelled in that split (live arbitrage pays the pool fee and grows the pool slightly). Run a pool's reserves and the price multiple you fear through my hub's post-move reserves calculator, so the inventory you would actually withdraw — and its value against holding — are numbers you know.
29. **Splitting a trade across pools can beat the best single pool** — a trade moves the price of the pool it trades against, so two smaller legs each move their pool less than one big leg moves one pool: the same 100 into two identical pools returns more than 100 into either alone. That is why aggregators split routes — and why the split is not 50/50 unless the pools are: deeper pools and lower-fee pools should take the larger share, because the optimum is where both legs' marginal rates meet. Splitting is not free in the real world — an extra leg costs extra transaction fees and can fail independently — so the modelled gain has to clear those costs to be worth taking. Run your trade size and two candidate pools through my hub's split-route swap planner, and compare its split against the best single-pool figure it also reports before assuming a routed trade is a better trade.
30. **Settle a CLMM position against holding, with the fees it actually earned** — item 13 gives the fee hurdle a price move creates for a concentrated position, but a hurdle alone is not a verdict: the settlement is fees earned minus that hurdle, and the position's value at the check price plus the fees against what simply holding the entry tokens would be worth. Report both bottom lines, because they can disagree in sign — a position at the top edge of its range can be up on its entry value while still behind holding, since holding kept all of the token that rose. At the entry price there is no hurdle at all, so any fees are pure gain against holding. Run your position's liquidity, range, entry and check prices and your actual fees earned through my hub's CLMM net return calculator, so whether concentrated liquidity beat doing nothing is a number you know, not a feeling from a green position value.
31. **Know the band your CLMM fees can defend** — item 22's tolerance band for a concentrated position is wider in spirit and stranger in shape: inside the range the shortfall vs holding grows one way, beyond an edge another, and on the downside it stops growing at all once the position is a single falling token — the most a fall can cost you against holding is the token B you held at entry, so fees above that figure cannot be consumed by any fall, while a rise can always outrun them because holding kept the whole token that rose. Whether the band's edges fall inside your range matters too: an edge beyond a range edge means your fees survive the position going fully single-sided on that side. Run your position's liquidity, range, entry price and fees earned so far through my hub's CLMM IL tolerance band, and you know exactly which prices your fees still cover — and which side runs out first.
32. **Plan a CLMM position by the volume it needs, not the volume you hope for** — item 16 puts a timescale on break-even at a given volume; the planning form inverts it: pick the price move you fear and the days you'd accept to recover from it, and the pool's required daily volume is forced — your fee hurdle (item 13) divided by the days, divided again by what your position actually captures of each traded unit (the fee tier × your share of the active liquidity × your time in range). Small shares and low tiers demand startling volumes: covering the 0.8–1.25 upper-edge hurdle in one day at a 10% share of a 0.25% pool takes ≈52,786 B of pool volume a day. And some plans have no answer at all — a zero fee tier or a position that is never in range earns nothing at any volume, which is worth knowing before the deposit, not after. Run your position, fee tier, share and deadline through my hub's CLMM required-volume planner, and compare the volume it reports against the pool's real trading on the pools page before you call a position viable.
33. **Plan a constant-product position by the volume it needs, too** — item 32's planning form for an ordinary AMM v4 / CPMM / CP-Swap position: pick the price move you fear and the days you'd accept to recover from it, and the pool's required daily volume is forced — the break-even hurdle on your deposit (the fees that must cover the impermanent loss, priced by my hub's break-even fee calculator) divided by the days, divided again by what you actually capture of each traded unit (the fee tier × your share of the pool). Small shares and low tiers demand startling volumes: covering a 2× move's hurdle on a 1,000 deposit in ten days at a 1% share of a 0.25% pool takes ≈343,146 a day of pool volume. And a zero fee tier earns nothing at any volume, which is worth knowing before the deposit, not after. Run your move, deposit, share, fee tier and deadline through my hub's constant-product required-volume planner, and compare the volume it reports against the pool's real trading on the pools page before you call a position viable.
34. **Entering a CLMM range from one token means letting the range set the swap** — a concentrated position's deposit split is not yours to choose and not the swap pool's ratio: at the current price your range demands a fixed amount of token B per token A (at the range's centre, exactly the price's worth — a value-balanced deposit; near the top edge, almost all B). So a single-sided entry swaps the amount that remaining-A × that ratio forces — on a balanced pool at price 1 in a 0.8–1.25 range, holding only A means swapping ≈51.3 of every 100 A before depositing, more than half, and a higher swap fee pushes the split higher still because the swap leg leaks. If the price is outside your range the answer is simpler and worth hearing before you pay for a swap: below the range the position is entirely the token you hold and no swap is needed; above it, everything must be swapped. Run your holding, your range and the pool you'd swap in through my hub's CLMM single-sided zap-in planner, and check the leftover it reports is the dust it should be — a plan that silently absorbs a real remainder is a plan that's wrong somewhere.
35. **Leaving a CLMM range into one token? Price the swap out of the other leg first** — closing a concentrated position pays whatever it holds at the exit price (item 10), and inside the range that is both tokens: ending up holding only one means swapping the other leg away, and that swap's fee plus price impact are a real exit cost — closing the 0.8–1.25 headline position at price 1 and consolidating in a balanced pool costs ≈4.65% of the pair's spot value at a 25 bps tier, less in a deeper pool. Outside the range the answer splits: at or below the lower edge the position is already entirely the token you'd keep, so there is nothing to swap and the cost is zero; at or above the top it is entirely the other token, so everything must be swapped and the cost is the swap's whole price impact. Run your position's liquidity, range and exit price plus the pool you'd swap in through my hub's CLMM single-sided zap-out planner before you close, so the cost of leaving in one token is a number you know, not a surprise in the fill.
36. **Plan a CLMM deposit from the token you actually hold first** — item 9's planner starts from a token-A amount, but a wallet often holds the other side first: if your funds sit in token B (the stablecoin side of the pair, say), the matching token A is set by the same range maths run in reverse — inside the range your B is worth L = amountB ÷ (√price − √lower) of liquidity, and the A that must sit alongside it follows from that L. Near the bottom of the range the B side is the small side, so a modest B deposit can demand a surprisingly large A match; at or above the top the position is entirely B and needs no A at all, while at or below the bottom it is entirely A and a B deposit cannot fund it at all. Run your price, range and token-B amount through my hub's CLMM token-B deposit planner before you deposit, so the match you need is a number you know, not a surprise at the deposit screen.
37. **Re-centre a position the price has drifted, don't just watch it drift** — a CLMM position's token mix is not the mix you deposited: as price moves inside the range it steadily converts into the other token (item 10), and outside the range it sits entirely in one token earning nothing. Re-centring — closing and reopening a range centred on the current price — resets the mix to 50/50 by value at that price, and it only ever takes one swap: the new liquidity is sized so the re-centred position is worth exactly what the old one is worth, so nothing is added or removed, and the swap is the difference between the drifted holdings and the centred target, priced at spot (a real swap also pays its fee and price impact — items 34 and 35 price that side). If the price has left your range entirely, exactly half the position's value swaps into the other token; if your old range already is the centred range at your chosen width, the honest answer is no swap at all. Run your position's liquidity, old range and current price plus the width you'd reopen at through my hub's CLMM re-centre / rebalance planner before you rebalance, so the swap and the new liquidity are numbers you know, not surprises at the confirmation screen.
38. **Size a CLMM exit before you make it — a partial withdrawal keeps the mix, not the ratio you deposited** — leaving a concentrated position is not the reverse of entering it at today's prices: you receive whatever the position holds at the price you exit at (item 10), which after a move is a different mix from your deposit. Withdrawing part of the position doesn't change that mix either — a position's holdings scale exactly with its liquidity, so taking out 50% of the liquidity pays 50% of the current holdings and leaves the other 50% in the same range with the same mix, only smaller; a 100% withdrawal is the full close and pays the position's holdings in full. If the price has left your range entirely, the exit is a single token — the other one isn't coming back at withdrawal, whatever you originally deposited. Fees the position has earned are collected on top of the withdrawal, not inside these amounts, so check them separately on the pool page before you judge an exit. Run your position's liquidity, range, exit price and the percentage you'd withdraw through my hub's CLMM withdrawal planner first, so both what you receive and what stays behind are numbers you know before you sign anything.
39. **Plan a constant-product deposit from your wallet, not from one token** — item 15's wallet question for an ordinary constant-product pool, where item 6's deposit ratio cuts the other way too: you rarely hold exactly the pair a pool wants, you hold fixed amounts of both tokens, and the deposit has to land in the pool's ratio — so the side that is scarcer *in ratio terms* (not the smaller balance) caps what you can deposit, is used in full, and leaves part of the other side behind in your wallet. Balances that happen to sit exactly in the pool's ratio are the only ones used in full. Mind the dust, too: amounts modelled at 9 decimal places floor, so a token-B-capped deposit can leave a few smallest units of B undeposited — exactly one unit (0.000000001) in the hub's pinned test case — harmless, but a plan that claims to use a balance to the last unit without mentioning it is rounding in its own favour. Run your pool's reserves and both of your balances through my hub's constant-product wallet-balance deposit planner before you deposit, so what goes in and what stays behind are numbers you know, not a leftover you discover afterwards.
40. **Price a routed trade backwards from the amount you need** — item 24 prices a routed trade from the amount you'd pay in, but the trades that matter usually start from the other end: you need an exact amount of a token (to repay, to settle, to fund a deposit) and the pair only exists through an intermediate. Price it backwards: the second pool's exact-out on your target says how much of the intermediate it must receive, and the first pool's exact-out on that amount says what you actually pay — each hop rounding its required input up, so the figure you budget is never a hair short in the model. The combined impact compounds just as it does forwards (on two balanced pools at 25 bps a hop, receiving exactly 100 takes ≈125.67 in, ≈20.4% combined impact), and some targets have no price at all: a pool can never pay out its whole reserve, so a target that would drain either pool on the route is impossible, not expensive. Run both pools' reserves, both fees and your exact target through my hub's two-hop exact-out swap model before you commit funds, so the amount in is a number you know, not a surprise at the confirmation screen.
41. **Ask how long break-even takes, in days, before you deposit** — items 13 and 16 give a constant-product position's two halves separately: the fees it must earn to match holding after a price move, and the fees a day of pool volume actually pays at your share. The decision lives in the quotient. A shortfall that takes three days of fees to cover is a different position from the same shortfall at three hundred days — and the second is usually a holding you should not be in, because the model behind the count assumes volume, fee tier, your share and the price all hold still, and over hundreds of days none of them will. Two edges are honest rather than comforting: no price move means no shortfall and a break-even of zero days, and a real shortfall at zero volume or a zero fee tier means never — not "a long time". Run your feared price move, deposit, the pool's daily volume, fee tier and your share of its TVL through my hub's constant-product break-even days calculator before you deposit, so the wait you are signing up for is a number you know.
42. **Need an exact amount? Split the target, don't just split the payment** — item 29's split runs forwards from an amount in, but an exact-out need (a repayment, a settlement, a deposit leg) runs backwards: the target is divided across two pools for the same pair so the total paid in is smallest, each leg priced by the exact-out maths of item 7's tool. On two identical 1,000/1,000 pools at 25 bps a target of exactly 100 splits about evenly and costs ≈105.53 in, against ≈111.39 from either pool alone — and the deeper or cheaper pool takes the larger share of the target, for the same marginal-rate reason item 29 gives. The case worth knowing before you need it: a target at or above one pool's whole reserve out has no single-pool price at all, but the two pools combined may still cover it — then splitting is not a saving, it is the only route — while a target at or above both reserves combined can never be paid out, because a constant-product pool never pays its whole reserve. Run both pools' reserves, both fees and your exact target through my hub's split-route exact-out swap model before you commit funds, and remember the modelled saving still has to clear the extra leg's network and transaction costs, which the model does not charge.
43. **A CLMM range is a wall, not a well** — in a constant-product pool a bigger trade just gets a worse price, all the way down; in a concentrated range the trade stops: paying token A walks the price down the range's curve, and at the lower edge the range holds no token B left to pay out, so the most a swap can ever take from one range is the token that range actually holds at the current price. Anything past that continues into the next tick range at whatever liquidity lives there — usually thinner, which is why a large CLMM swap's real fill can fall off a cliff the single-range picture doesn't show. Before sizing a trade against a concentrated pool, run the range's liquidity (my hub's CLMM range deposit planner reports it), the range, the current price and your amount through my hub's CLMM single-range swap model: if the model says your input is partly unfilled, the live trade is really several trades across several ranges, and only the first is priced the way you modelled.
44. **The second range sets the cliff** — item 43's wall has a far side: a swap too big for the active range crosses into the next tick range and keeps walking there, at that range's own liquidity — and ranges away from the current price usually hold less of it. So the same crossing trade gets two different fills depending on what lives past the edge: against a second range as deep as the first, a 200-in trade at the 0.8–1.25 centre fills in full and lands near price 0.68; against one a tenth as deep it fills both ranges and stops at the second wall with over a third of the input unused; against one ten times as deep it barely steps past the edge at all. 53. **APR is not APY — compounding is the difference, and it rests on an assumption** — the fee estimators annualise a day's fees by ×365 and call the result, honestly, a naive APR; the settlement tools count fees held outside the position. What neither shows is what happens if the fees go back in and start earning themselves: at n reinvestments a year the balance grows by (1 + APR/n)^(n·t), and the effective APY, (1 + APR/n)^n − 1, sits above the APR whenever both are positive — a 25% APR compounded daily is a ≈28.3916% APY, compounded yearly it is exactly 25%, and no frequency pushes it past the continuous limit e^APR − 1. But the assumption doing the work deserves more attention than the formula: the APR is held constant on a growing balance, and in a live pool it will not be — volume fades, TVL arrives, your share dilutes, a CLMM position drifts out of range — while impermanent loss runs the whole time and each reinvestment costs a transaction and re-enters at the pool's then-current ratio. Treat any APY figure as an upper-bound sketch of a rate that will move, not a yield you were promised. 54. **A flat end price does not mean a free trip — LVR charges for the path** — impermanent loss compares your position with holding at the end price only, so a token that rises 10% and falls straight back shows zero IL and looks harmless. It was not: on the way up, arbitrageurs bought from your pool at yesterday's price; on the way down they sold back into it the same way, and on both legs the difference between what the pool held and what a portfolio rebalancing at the external price would hold — loss-versus-rebalancing — came out of the LP side. For a round trip of multiple m the cost is Rb·(√m − 1)² on the way up and that divided by √m on the way down (≈4.6537 B per trip on a 1,000/1,000 pool for a ±10% excursion, ≈0.2327% of the position — and the reserves afterwards are exactly the starting reserves, so the same trip can charge you again tomorrow). That is why two pools with the same start and end prices can leave their LPs in different places: volatility itself is a cost, separate from where price settles. Two honest qualifications: the model is gross of fees, and in a live pool arbitrageurs pay the swap fee, part of which reaches LPs and offsets some LVR; and real paths are many unequal steps, not identical round trips. 103. **Slice a large swap over time only when the pool gets to reset between slices** — one trade walks the whole curve at once, which is why tool 1's price impact grows with size; cutting the trade into equal slices (a TWAP-style execution) helps only because, between slices, trading against the external price restores the pool toward its starting spot, so each slice walks the same short stretch instead of the whole trade walking it once. The numbers on a 1,000/1,000 pool at a 0.25% fee: paying 100 at once returns ≈90.70243237 (impact ≈9.2976%); ten slices of 10 return ≈98.76482091 in total (+≈8.8888%, impact ≈1.2352%), and as the slices multiply the impact falls toward the fee itself, never below it. Read the conditions before you slice: the gain assumes the external price still equals the starting spot when each slice lands — if the price moves while you wait, later slices price at the new market; each slice is a separate transaction paying its own network and priority fees, which the model does not charge, so more slices is not free; and at dust sizes the per-slice rounding to the smallest unit can cost more than the impact saved, which the model shows as a negative gain rather than hiding. Run your reserves, total amount, slice count and fee through my hub's sliced (TWAP-style) swap planner before you split a trade: the gain, the impact and the dust loss are numbers, not vibes. Your reserves, amount, slices and fee, not live pool data; not a live quote; not financial advice.
104. **Count the network's cut before you count a strategy's gain** — every pool tool on the hub prices the pool's maths and stops at the pool's edge, but each real transaction also pays Solana itself, and strategies that multiply transactions (item 103's slices, item 29's split routes, item 98's two-pool cycles) multiply that cut too. The arithmetic is the protocol's own: a base fee of 5,000 lamports per signature, plus a prioritisation fee of the compute-unit price (in micro-lamports per CU) times the compute-unit limit, divided by 1,000,000 and rounded up. Two habits follow. First, the priority fee is charged on the limit you request, not the compute you consume — a 1,400,000 CU limit at 10,000 micro-lamports pays 14,000 lamports even if the transaction uses a tenth of it, so a tight, measured limit is cheaper than a loose one at the same price. Second, keep rent out of the fee column: creating a token account locks a rent-exempt deposit (2,039,280 lamports for the standard 165-byte account) that comes back when the account closes — it is upfront capital, not a cost, though a rent-reduction schedule (SIMD-0437) is stepping the figure down and Token-2022 accounts with extensions are larger. The ten-slice example makes the scale concrete: ten transactions at one signature, a 300,000 CU limit and 10,000 micro-lamports per CU pay 8,000 lamports each — 80,000 lamports (0.00008 SOL) of fees, plus 2,039,280 lamports of deposit if the first slice opens a new token account. Fees are also owed on transactions that fail, and the CU price that actually lands a transaction moves with congestion, so treat any figure here as a model at your price input, not a quote. Run your transaction count, signatures, compute limit, CU price and new-account count through my hub's Solana transaction cost estimator before you execute a multi-transaction plan, so the network's cut is a number next to the strategy's modelled gain — not a surprise under it. Your counts and price inputs, not live network data; not a live quote; not financial advice.
105. **A stableswap hedge removes small-move direction and keeps the depeg** — delta-hedging a stableswap position works exactly like hedging any other pool position (item 100's identity: short the position's token-A holding and its price delta is zero at today's price), but the stableswap curve defends par, so the moves a hedge cannot help with are precisely the ones that matter. While both tokens hold their peg the position barely moves and the hedge barely matters; if one side truly depegs, arbitrage pays for the new price with almost the whole reserve of the depegging side — on a balanced pool at amplification 100, a doubling of token A's price leaves the pool holding about 68 of its 1,000 A, and a hedged 10% share settles about 43.4% down where the same hedge on a constant-product position costs about 8.6%. Higher amplification deepens that shape rather than softening it. Two habits follow: size a stableswap hedge for the depeg scenario, not for ordinary wiggles, and expect to re-hedge drastically after any real move, because the position's token-A holding — the hedge itself — collapses as the curve surrenders the reserve. Run your reserves, amplification, share and check price through my hub's stableswap delta-hedge calculator before treating a stable-pool position as hedged. Your reserves, amplification, share and price move, not live pool data; not a live quote; not financial advice.

106. **Leverage inside a range can make break-even unreachable** — borrowing against a constant-product position (item 102) multiplies a curve that never stops rising, but a concentrated position's value stops at the top of its range: past the upper edge it is entirely the quote token and worth no more at any higher price. So a levered CLMM position has a break-even target — your equity plus the debt as interest grows it — that can sit above the most the position can ever be worth. On a 0.8–1.25 range entered at 1 with 100 B at 3x and a 10% borrow APR, a year grows the 200 B debt to 220 B and the target to 320 B, against a ceiling of about 317.7 B: no rise, however large, repays the debt with the equity intact, and at the top edge the levered equity is down about 2.3% on a move the unlevered position makes +5.9% from. Two habits follow: before levering a range, compare the break-even target with the position's all-quote value at the top edge — if the target is above it, the loan can only be repaid out of fees, never out of price — and remember the downside has no such cap, because below the range the position is entirely the base token and falls linearly (the same 3x position liquidates at about 0.77x and wipes out at about 0.69x). Run your range, entry, equity, leverage and borrow terms through my hub's leveraged CLMM position model before treating a levered range as a bigger version of an unlevered one. Your range, entry, equity, leverage and borrow terms, not live pool data; not a live quote; not financial advice.

107. **Compare the direct pool with the routed path** — a swap does not have to go straight from the token you hold to the token you want: routing it through an intermediate token crosses two pools instead of one, and whether that helps is a depth question, not a loyalty question. The routed trade pays a second fee and takes price impact twice, so it only wins when the hop pools are deep enough that the impact they save is worth more than the fee they add. On my hub's numbers, 100 paid into a direct pool holding 1,000 / 1,000 at a 0.25% fee pays about 90.7024, while the same 100 routed through two pools holding 10,000 / 10,000 each at 0.25% per hop pays about 97.5568 — roughly 7.56% more — but routed through two pools the same size as the direct one it pays only about 82.9690, because nothing about the shallower hops earns back the second fee. Two cautions before trusting any such comparison: the two routes are priced against the same starting state, so if they share a pool (the direct pool doubling as a hop leg is common) executing one route would move the other; and a real router can split a trade across both routes at once and charges each route its own network fees, which a side-by-side comparison leaves out. Run your pools, amount and fees through my hub's swap route comparison model before assuming the direct pool is the best execution — or that the routed one is. Your pools, amount and fees, not live pool data; not a live quote; not financial advice.

108. **Price a target output through both routes** — when you need an exact amount of a token, the route question flips: not "which route pays more for my input?" but "which route charges less for my target?" Price the purchase, not the sale. On my hub's numbers, receiving exactly 90 from a direct pool holding 1,000 / 1,000 at a 0.25% fee costs about 99.1490 in, while routing the purchase through two pools holding 10,000 / 10,000 each at 0.25% per hop costs about 92.1118 — the deeper hops' lower impact saves about 7.10% of the direct route's cost, more than the second fee takes. Route the same purchase through two pools the size of the direct one and it costs about 110.3373 instead: the fee and the impact are charged twice on the way back too, and shallow hops never earn that back. Two cautions carry over from comparing routes forwards: the target has to be smaller than every reserve it is drawn against, because a constant-product pool can never pay out its whole reserve — a route that would drain a pool is not expensive, it is impossible — and the routes are priced against the same starting state, so a pool the two routes share is counted twice. Each exact-out leg also rounds the input it requires up, so a routed purchase rounds twice; that is the honest price of a guaranteed target, and it is already inside the numbers above. Run your pools, target and fees through my hub's exact-out route comparison model before assuming the direct pool is the cheapest way to buy an exact amount — or that the routed one is. Your pools, target and fees, not live pool data; not a live quote; not financial advice.

109. **Price a swap through two intermediate tokens** — some pairs have no usable route with a single hop in the middle: the token you hold trades into one liquid intermediate, that intermediate into a second, and only then into the token you want. Each added hop pays its own pool's fee and takes its own price impact, and the impacts compound rather than add, so a three-hop route is only worth its extra crossing when every pool on it is deep. On my hub's numbers, 100 through three pools holding 1,000 / 1,000 each at a 0.25% fee per hop pays about 90.7024, then about 82.9690, then about 76.4356 — a combined impact of about 23.56% — while the same 100 through three pools holding 10,000 / 10,000 each pays about 96.3751, a combined impact of about 3.62%. Read a long route by its shallowest pool: that pool sets the route's ceiling, and deepening the other two cannot fix it. Two cautions: the hops are priced in order against the reserves you start with, so a real route is exposed to other trades moving the later pools while the early hops settle; and each hop pays its own network and priority fees on top of the pool fees, which adds up fastest exactly on the long routes that need them least. Run your pools, amount and fees through my hub's three-hop swap model before assuming a two-intermediate route is executable at anything like its quoted spot — or that it is not. 110. **Price a target output through two intermediate tokens** — when the pair you need has no usable route with a single hop in the middle and you need an exact amount out, the purchase has to be priced backwards, pool by pool: the last pool's exact-out on your target says how much of the second intermediate it must receive, that amount's exact-out in the middle pool says how much of the first intermediate it must receive, and that amount's exact-out in the first pool is what you pay. Each hop rounds the input it requires up, so a three-hop purchase rounds three times — that is the honest price of a guaranteed target, and it is already inside the numbers. On my hub's numbers, receiving exactly 50 through three pools holding 1,000 / 1,000 each at a 0.25% fee per hop costs about 59.2932 in — the hops must receive about 55.8422 and about 52.7635 — a combined impact of about 15.67%, while the same 50 through three pools holding 10,000 / 10,000 each costs about 51.1460, a combined impact of about 2.24%. Feasibility bites at every hop, not just the last: the target must be smaller than the final pool's reserve of it, and each intermediate amount worked backwards to must be smaller than the reserve it is drawn from — a route that would drain any pool on it cannot buy the target at any price. And the cautions from pricing the same route forwards carry over: the hops are priced against the reserves you start with, so a real route is exposed to other trades moving the later pools, and each hop pays its own network and priority fees on top of the pool fees. Run your pools, target and fees through my hub's three-hop exact-out swap model before assuming an exact amount is buyable through two intermediates — or that the price you are quoted for it is fair. 111. **Compare every route length, not just the shortest** — once a pair can be reached directly, through one intermediate token, and through two, "which route?" is really "which length?": each added hop charges its pool's fee and takes its price impact again, so a longer route only earns its crossings when every pool on it is deep enough that the impact it saves beats the fees it adds. On my hub's numbers, paying 100 into a direct pool holding 1,000 / 1,000 at a 0.25% fee pays about 90.7024; routing through two pools holding 10,000 / 10,000 each pays about 97.5568 and wins; adding a third deep pool pays about 96.3751 — ahead of the direct pool, but the third hop hands back about 1.1817, so the middle route keeps the win by about 1.23% over the runner-up. Make every pool the same 1,000 / 1,000 size and the order flips completely — direct about 90.7024, two-hop about 82.9690, three-hop about 76.4356 — because shallow hops never earn back the extra crossings. Two cautions before trusting any such ranking: the routes are priced against the same starting state, so a pool the routes share — and routes from the same wallet usually share at least one — is counted once per route, while executing one route would move the others; and a real router can split a trade across several routes at once, while each route also pays its own network and priority fees, which grow with the route's length. Neither is in the comparison. Run your pools, amount and fees through my hub's route-length comparison model before assuming the shortest route is the best execution — or that the longest one is. 112. **Price the platform layer before you sign** — a pool's fee tier is not the whole price of a swap. An interface in front of the pool can charge its own platform fee on top, taken from the amount you pay in, in the token you pay, before the rest reaches the pool. On my hub's numbers, paying 100 into a pool holding 1,000 / 1,000 at a 0.25% pool fee pays about 90.7024 with no platform fee; add a disclosed 0.5% platform fee and exactly 0.5 goes to the interface, 99.5 reaches the pool, and you receive about 90.2899 — the platform layer costs about 0.4126, roughly 0.4549% of what you would have received, a little less than the headline rate because the slightly smaller trade also takes slightly less price impact. That is the shape an honest fee has: itemised before you sign, in the token it is charged in, with the wallet it goes to named. On an interface I build, that wallet is my own SOL address, labelled up front — and a fee you cannot get itemised like this is a fee to walk away from. Two edges worth knowing: the model's fee is rounded down to the smallest unit it can state, so a small enough trade can model a fee of exactly zero where a real interface rounding up, or charging a minimum, would take more; and a platform fee charged on the output side, or as a flat amount, prices differently and is not in this model — check which side of your trade any real interface charges before you compare its quote with a pool's. Run your pool, amount and both fees through my hub's platform-fee swap model before you sign a swap on any interface, so the layer between you and the pool is a number, not a surprise. Your pool, amount and fees, not live pool data; not a live quote; not financial advice.
113. **Quote the platform fee on an exact amount out** — item 112 prices the platform layer when you fix what you pay in; when you fix what you must receive, the layer prices the other way and the shape changes in one honest respect. The amount that has to reach the pool is the exact-out charge with no platform fee at all, and the total you pay is that amount grossed up for the platform fee — so the extra you pay over the no-fee total is exactly the fee itself, with no price-impact discount, because the pool receives the identical amount either way. On my hub's numbers, exactly 90 out of a pool holding 1,000 / 1,000 at a 0.25% pool fee and a 0.5% platform fee costs 99.647207367 in total: 0.498236036 to the interface, 99.148971331 to the pool — the fee is 0.5% of everything you pay, which reads as about 0.5025% measured on top of the smaller no-fee total, and both readings should be on the quote you sign. Watch the rate itself as it grows: the gross-up divides by what survives the fee, so at a 99.99% platform rate a target the pool would charge 3 smallest units for needs 20,001 units in total — a disclosed rate can still be a rate to walk away from. And because the exact-out charge rounds up to the smallest unit, the pool can pay a few of those units past your target; a careful interface shows that excess rather than rounding it away. Run your pool, exact target and both fees through my hub's platform-fee exact-out swap model before you sign, so the total — not just the target — is a number you chose. Your pool, target and fees, not live pool data; not a live quote; not financial advice.

114. **Estimate what the interface itself earns** — items 112 and 113 price the platform layer from the trader's side; the operator's side is the same disclosed fee read as revenue over time, and it deserves the same itemised honesty. A day's revenue is the day's fee-bearing volume times the rate, no more: on my hub's numbers, 100,000 of volume a day at a 0.5% platform fee earns 500 a day and 15,000 over 30 days, and against a daily operating cost of 100 the period nets 12,000, with break-even at 20,000 of volume a day — the smallest daily volume whose day's fee reaches the daily cost. Two cautions keep the projection honest. First, the model applies the fee once to the day's whole volume, while a real interface taking its fee trade by trade rounds every trade down to the smallest unit — so what it actually collects is never more than the aggregate figure, and usually a little less on a day of many small trades; read the model as the ceiling, not the deposit. Second, everything is held constant — volume, rate and costs — so the naive annual run-rate (182,500 on these numbers, the daily revenue times 365) assumes every future day matches the one modelled, which no real interface's days do; growth, decline, refunds and volume served fee-free are not in it. A fee that can be estimated this plainly, in the open, is the kind an interface should be charging; revenue that only works when the rate is hidden is not a business model, it is a leak. 115. **Compare two fee tiers before you set one** — item 114 prices a single disclosed platform rate as revenue over time; choosing that rate is the step before, and it is a comparison, not a maximisation. A higher rate earns more per unit of volume and may keep less of it, and this model refuses to guess the trade-off: you supply the daily volume you expect at each of two rates, and it prices both in the same floored arithmetic. On my hub's numbers, 100,000 of volume a day at 0.25% earns 250 a day, while 80,000 a day at 0.5% earns 400 a day — the higher rate wins on a fifth less volume — and the break-even volumes turn that into the question to actually test: the 0.5% tier matches the lower tier's revenue on just 50,000 of volume a day, so it only has to keep half the flow to win, while the 0.25% tier would need 160,000 a day to match it. A tier charging nothing earns nothing at any volume, and when the other tier earns anything the model reports that there is no catch-up volume rather than inventing one. How volume really answers a rate change is your interface's own experiment — no elasticity is assumed here, and a rate chosen because a model assumed the volume would stay is a rate chosen on a hope. Run both tiers — each rate with the volume you honestly expect at it — through my hub's interface fee-tier comparison model before you set a rate, so the choice is a number you chose, not a default you inherited. 116. **Split the platform fee with a referrer in the open** — item 115 compares two disclosed platform rates; once a rate is set, the next honest question is who the fee pays. Interfaces grow when the people who bring traders share in the fee those traders pay, and that split deserves the same itemised honesty as the fee itself: the trader pays one disclosed total, the referrer takes an agreed share of the platform fee, and the builder keeps the rest — a division of the fee, never a second charge stacked on the trade. The arithmetic is exact and small enough to check by hand: the referrer's cut is the fee times the share, rounded down to the smallest unit, and the builder keeps the remainder, so the two cuts always sum to the fee itself; on my hub's numbers a 0.5 fee at a 25% referrer share pays the referrer 0.125 and leaves the builder 0.375, and the trader's payout does not move by a single unit at any share. Two habits keep a split honest. First, always state the share as a share of the fee, and the effective rate beside it — 25% of a 0.5% fee is 0.125% of the trade, and a split described only as '25%' invites the reader to fear it is 25% of their money. Second, decide the dust rule before the first trade: at small sizes the referrer's rounded-down cut can be exactly zero, so say whether the builder keeps that remainder (my hub's model does) or the interface rounds the referrer up or pays a minimum — any of the three is fair, and only the unstated one is not. 117. **Split the platform fee with a referrer when the amount out is fixed** — item 116 splits the disclosed platform fee when the amount paid in is fixed; a trader who needs an exact quantity out reverses the question, and the split has to survive the reversal unchanged. It does, because the split divides the fee and nothing else: the total you pay is the platform-fee exact-out model's own grossed-up charge for your target, and only then is its fee divided — the referrer's cut is the fee times the share, rounded down to the smallest unit, and the builder keeps the remainder, so the two cuts always sum to the fee itself. On my hub's numbers — balanced 1,000/1,000 reserves, exactly 90 to receive at a 0.25% pool fee and a disclosed 0.5% platform fee — the total is 99.647207367, the fee is 0.498236036, and a 25% referrer share pays the referrer 0.124559009 while the builder keeps 0.373677027; the total and the payout do not move by a single unit at any share. The habits from the exact-in split carry over word for word: state the share as a share of the fee with the effective rate beside it (25% of a 0.5% fee is about 0.125% of the total paid in, never 25% of the trade), and settle the dust rule in advance, because at small targets the referrer's rounded-down cut can be exactly zero while the builder keeps the whole tiny fee. A day's fee is the day's fee-bearing volume times the rate, and only then is it divided: the referrer's cut is the fee times the share, rounded down to the smallest unit, and the builder keeps the remainder, so the two cuts sum to the fee itself on every day, over the whole period, and in the naive annual run-rates. 119. **Cover the builder's costs after the referral split** — item 118 splits the fee revenue over time, but a split is not a living until the builder's own costs come off the builder's cut: the referrer's cut is the referrer's, and netting your hosting and operating costs against it on paper is how a partnership quietly goes bad. So cost the two sides separately: the builder's cut is what remains of the fee after the referrer is paid, the period's costs come off that cut alone, and the net is signed — a period can come out short after the split even when the unsplit fee would have covered the cost, and an honest plan says the shortfall out loud instead of rounding it to zero. The volume question changes with the split too: the break-even volume is the smallest daily volume whose builder cut alone covers the daily cost, and because the builder keeps only part of the fee it is always at least the unsplit break-even at the same rate and cost, and higher the larger the referrer's share — at a full share to the referrer, or at a zero rate, there is no break-even volume at any traffic, and the plan should say so rather than invent one. On my hub's numbers — 100,000 of volume a day at a 0.5% platform fee with a 25% referrer share and a daily cost of 100 — the day's fee of 500 splits 125 / 375, the builder nets 275 a day after the cost, 11,250 of builder cut over 30 days against 3,000 of costs nets 8,250, and the builder's cut alone breaks even from 26,666.6666666 of volume a day, one smallest unit less than which the builder falls strictly short. 120. **Compare two referral tiers by the builder's cut** — when two offers are on the table, each is a rate, a referrer share and the volume that pairing is expected to bring, and ranking them by the platform fee they collect ranks the wrong wallet: the builder lives on what remains after the referrer is paid, and a tier can collect more fee overall while leaving its builder less, because the referrer's share comes out of the larger fee first. So compare the builder cuts themselves, tier by tier, with each tier's split worked exactly — the day's fee is the volume times the rate floored at the smallest unit, the referrer's cut is that fee times the share floored, and the builder keeps the remainder, so the cuts sum exactly to the fee.

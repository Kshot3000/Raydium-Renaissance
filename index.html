Raydium liquidity pools, in 30 seconds

  • A pool is a pair of token vaults. A Raydium liquidity pool holds two tokens (say SOL and USDC). Traders swap against the pool; liquidity providers (LPs) deposit both sides and earn a share of the trading fees.
  • Constant product (AMM v4 / CPMM). The classic pool type prices swaps with x × y = k — the larger your trade against the reserves, the worse your price. That curve is exactly what Tool 1 below models.
  • CLMM = concentrated liquidity. CLMM pools let LPs place liquidity inside a chosen price range, earning more fees while the price stays in range — and nothing when it leaves it. Capital-efficient, but it needs active range management.
  • CP-Swap = the newer standard pool. Raydium's CP-Swap pools use constant-product maths too, without the old OpenBook market requirement, and support Token-2022 tokens.
  • Fees vary by pool. Each pool carries its own fee tier; a configured share of each swap fee goes to LPs in proportion to their share of the pool. Always check the fee tier on the actual pool page before depositing.
  • The risk has a name: impermanent loss. If one token's price moves against the other, an LP can end up with less than simply holding — Tool 2 quantifies it. Fees earned are what compensate LPs for taking that risk.

Pool tools — right here, nothing leaves your browser

Honest labels: these are educational models of how Raydium-style pools behave, using example numbers you type in. They are not live quotes, not live pool data, and not financial advice. For real pools, use raydium.io/liquidity-pools.

1 · Constant-product swap model

The x × y = k maths behind AMM v4 / CPMM pools. Enter a pool's reserves and a trade size; the model takes the fee off the input, then prices the swap on the curve.

2 · Impermanent-loss calculator

For a 50/50 constant-product position: if one token's price moves by a multiple against the other, how far behind simply holding do you end up — before fees?

3 · LP fee estimator

Your slice of a pool's swap fees is your share of its TVL. Enter a pool's 24h volume and TVL and your deposit to estimate daily fees — assuming nothing changes, which it always does.

4 · Break-even fee calculator

Fees are what pay an LP back for impermanent loss. For the same 50/50 constant-product position as Tool 2, this shows exactly how much in fees you must earn to match simply holding — and, if you add your estimated daily fees from Tool 3, how many days that would take at that rate.

5 · Liquidity deposit planner

Before the maths in Tools 1–4 matters, a constant-product pool (AMM v4 / CPMM / CP-Swap) takes both tokens in the pool's existing ratio. Enter a pool's reserves and how much of token A you want to add — the planner shows the matching token B amount and your share of the pool afterwards. CLMM deposits are range-based and work differently.

6 · Exact-out swap model

The inverse of Tool 1: instead of asking what a trade gets you, enter the amount out you actually want and the model works backwards along x × y = k for the amount in you'd need — grossed up for the fee and rounded up so the input is never short. A target equal to or above the whole reserve out is rejected: a constant-product pool can never pay out everything it holds.

7 · Liquidity withdrawal planner

The exit side of Tool 5: withdrawing from a constant-product pool (AMM v4 / CPMM / CP-Swap) pays out both tokens in the pool's current ratio, in proportion to the share you redeem. Enter the pool's current reserves and your share of the pool — the planner shows what a full or partial withdrawal returns, and what you'd have left. If prices have moved since you deposited, the mix you get back differs from what you put in — that's impermanent loss made concrete. CLMM withdrawals are range-based and work differently.

8 · CLMM range deposit planner

Tools 5 and 7 are constant-product only — a CLMM position works differently: you pick a price range, and where the current price sits inside it sets the token split for you. Enter a current price and your range (token B per token A) plus the token A you want to deposit — the planner shows the matching token B, the model's liquidity, and the range's tick indices. Below your range a position is entirely token A and earns nothing until price enters it; at or above the top it is entirely token B, so a token-A deposit can't fund it. Tick indices here are modelled with floating-point logs and are not snapped to a pool's tick spacing.

9 · CLMM position checker

The follow-up to Tool 8: a CLMM position's token mix is not fixed — as the price moves, the position steadily converts itself. Enter the model liquidity Tool 8 reported for your deposit, your range, and a price to check — the checker shows what the position holds at that price. Inside the range a rising price converts the position into token B (you end up holding less of the token that rose — concentrated liquidity's version of impermanent loss); at or outside either edge the position is entirely one token and earns no fees until price returns.

10 · Slippage & minimum-received calculator

Every swap and liquidity add carries a slippage tolerance: how far the result may move against you before the transaction fails instead of filling badly. Enter the amount out you expect (Tools 1 and 6 model it) and your tolerance — the calculator shows your minimum received, floored and never rounded up: a minimum that rounds up is stricter than the tolerance you set, and a maximum input that rounds up lets you pay more than you authorised. Add your expected amount in to see the maximum input the same tolerance implies.

11 · CLMM tick / price converter

Tools 8 and 9 report CLMM ranges as tick indices — this is what a tick is. CLMM prices are discrete: each tick is one 0.01% price step (price = 1.0001tick), and a price's tick is that step counted down from 1, floored. Enter a price to see its tick and the tick's own price — and add your pool's tick spacing to see where a range boundary would snap: pools only accept boundaries on spacing multiples, a lower boundary snapping down and an upper one up. Tick maths here uses floating-point logs, so a price exactly on a boundary can land one tick off.

12 · CLMM position vs holding

Tool 9 shows what a CLMM position holds at a price — this is Tool 2's impermanent-loss question for that position. Enter the model liquidity Tool 8 reported, your range, the price you entered at, and a price to check: the calculator values the position at the check price against simply holding the tokens it started with, and shows the fees (in token B) it must have earned to break even with holding. The position converts itself as price moves, so it never beats holding on value alone — the gap is zero at the entry price and grows the further price travels.

13 · CLMM fee estimator

Tool 12 tells you the fees a CLMM position must earn to match holding — this estimates the fees it might actually earn. A concentrated position's share is not of the pool's TVL but of the active liquidity at the current tick, and it earns only while price is inside its range: your fees ≈ pool volume × fee tier × (your liquidity ÷ total active liquidity) × your time in range. The total active liquidity is an estimate you supply — it must be at least your own liquidity, since yours is part of it. Pair the daily figure with Tool 12's fee hurdle before trusting a range.

14 · CLMM wallet-balance deposit planner

Tool 8 asks how much token B a chosen token-A deposit needs — this asks the wallet's question instead: you hold a fixed amount of both tokens, so what is the biggest CLMM position those balances can fund in your range, and what is left over? Inside the range the scarcer side caps the position and is used in full; the other side is partly left unused. Below the range the position is entirely token A, at or above the top entirely token B, so the other balance is left over whole.

15 · CLMM break-even days calculator

Tools 12 and 13 leave the CLMM break-even as two numbers on two forms: the fees a position must earn to match holding at a moved price, and the fees it might earn per day. This divides one by the other — Tool 4's question for a CLMM position. Enter the position (liquidity, range, entry and check prices) and the fee side (total active liquidity, daily volume, fee tier, time in range): the result is how many days at that fee rate close the gap holding opens. At the entry price there is no gap, so the answer is 0 days; with a real gap and a zero fee rate the answer is honestly never.

16 · Constant-product arbitrage model

Why a pool's price tracks the wider market: if the pool's spot price (reserves B ÷ A) differs from a price elsewhere, a trade sized to move the pool's price exactly to that external price is the textbook arbitrage — and arbitrageurs making it is what pulls the pool back into line. Enter the pool's reserves and an external price you supply (this tool has no live feed and finds no opportunities): the result is the direction, the price-aligning trade size after the pool fee, and the modelled profit valued in B at your external price. A gap smaller than the fee honestly comes out unprofitable. Educational model only — not live quotes, not a found opportunity, not financial advice.

17 · Price-impact trade sizer

The inverse of tool 1: instead of asking what a trade gets, ask how large a trade can get before its price impact crosses a cap you choose. Enter the pool's reserves (in the direction you'd trade — what you pay in, what you receive) and your maximum acceptable price impact: the result is the largest pay-in that stays at that cap, and what it returns. Note the model's honest floor — tool 1's impact measure includes the pool fee, so a cap at or below the fee itself admits no positive trade at all, and the same cap in a deeper pool allows a proportionally larger trade. Educational model only — reserves are your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

18 · LP-token share & value calculator

Tools 5 and 7 speak in share percentages, but what you actually hold in a constant-product pool (AMM v4 / CPMM / CP-Swap) is a count of LP tokens — and that count against the pool's total LP supply is your share. Enter the pool's current reserves, its total LP supply and your token count: the calculator shows your share, what redeeming those tokens returns in both tokens (the exit Tool 7 plans, from the count you hold), and the position's value in token B at the pool's own spot price. One catch the percentages hide: your share shrinks as new LPs deposit and the supply grows, so a count that was 1% at deposit need not be 1% now. CLMM positions are range-based NFTs, not fungible LP tokens. Educational model only — reserves and supply are your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

19 · Single-sided zap-in planner

Tool 5 assumes you already hold both tokens in the pool's ratio — but a wallet often holds only one side. The standard way in from token A alone is a zap: swap part of your A for B in the same pool, then deposit the A that remains together with all the B the swap returned. Enter the pool's reserves, the token A you hold and the pool fee — the planner solves for the one split where the deposit matches the pool's post-swap ratio, so nothing is left over: too small a swap leaves you short of B, too large a swap leaves B unused. One honest invariant: the B comes straight back in as the deposit, so the pool's B reserve ends where it started. Educational model only — one pool, no routing, no price movement between the swap and the deposit (a real zap is a single transaction for exactly that reason); reserves are your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice. CLMM entries are range-based and differ.

20 · Single-sided zap-out planner

Tool 19's exit mirror. Tool 7's withdrawal always pays both tokens in the pool's ratio — but you may want to leave holding only one token. The standard way out to token A alone is a zap-out: withdraw, then swap all the token B the withdrawal returned back into A in the same pool. Enter the pool's reserves, your share of the pool, how much of your position to withdraw and the pool fee — both legs are tool 7's and tool 1's own models run against the post-withdrawal reserves, and the result prices the consolidation honestly: the fee plus the price impact of swapping into a pool your own withdrawal just made shallower, a cost that grows with your share. One honest edge: withdrawing all of a pool you own entirely leaves no pool behind to swap in, so that combination is reported as not completable in this pool rather than given a made-up fill. Educational model only — one pool, no routing, no price movement between the withdrawal and the swap (a real zap-out is a single transaction for exactly that reason), no withdrawal fee modelled; reserves are your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice. CLMM exits are range-based and differ.

21 · IL tolerance band

Tools 2 and 4 work forwards — a price move in, impermanent loss and break-even fees out. This one works backwards: given the fees your position has already earned, how far can the price still move before impermanent loss consumes those fees exactly? Enter your position's value and the fees earned so far, both in token B terms — the answer is a tolerance band: the price can multiply by the upper edge or fall to the lower edge before holding would have been better. The band is symmetric in the square root of the price, which is why the percentage moves differ: fees of 2% of the position buy +44% / −36% of room, and fees of 12.5% buy a rise to 2.25× or a fall to a quarter. One honest asymmetry: a fall can never cost more than half the position against holding — the half held in the other token is untouched — so fees of 50% of the position or more can never be consumed by a fall, however far the price drops; that side is reported as unbounded rather than given a made-up edge, while a rise has no such cap. Zero fees earned means zero room: the band has already collapsed to the entry price. Educational model only — a 50/50 constant-product position, fees counted in token B terms and held outside the pool, no compounding or fee growth of the position modelled; your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice. CLMM positions are range-based and differ.

22 · CLMM ±% range planner

Tools 8 and 14 ask for a CLMM range as two raw prices — but the way a range is usually chosen is a width around the current price: “±25%”. Enter the current price, a width, and the token A you want to deposit: the planner builds the range multiplicatively symmetric (upper = price × multiple, lower = price ÷ multiple — the kind of symmetry where a rise and a fall undo each other) and plans the deposit with Tool 8's own maths. The percentage room honestly differs by side: a ±25% width is +25% of room up but only −20% down, because the lower edge is price ÷ 1.25, not price × 0.75. Add your pool's tick spacing and the edges are snapped the way pools require — lower down, upper up — before planning, so the split and liquidity describe the snapped range a real position would actually cover, not the one you typed. Educational model only — no fees modelled; your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

23 · Two-hop swap model

Tool 1 models one pool — but many pairs have no direct pool, so a real trade routes through an intermediate token: token A → token M in one pool, then M → token C in a second. This model runs Tool 1's own maths twice: hop 2's input is hop 1's output exactly as Tool 1 reports it (floored at 9 decimal places — that, not a hidden-precision value, is what the second pool would receive). Each hop pays its own pool's fee and takes its own price impact against its own reserves, so the combined impact — measured against the product of the two pools' spot prices — is always worse than either hop alone, and a shallow second pool can dominate the whole route. Educational model only — both pools are constant-product here; a real route may cross CLMM pools or other venues, and no routing search is done: these are the two pools you typed, with your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

24 · Net LP return calculator

Tools 2 and 4 give the two halves separately — what the position is worth vs holding at a price move, and the fees it must earn to break even. This settles the question with the fees you actually earned: enter the price multiple, your deposit and your fees earned, and the calculator adds the fees to the position and gives the verdict against holding, both in $ and as a %. It also reports the return against the deposit itself — a different bottom line, reported alongside rather than instead: a position can be up on its deposit and still behind holding (at a 2× move with no fees, +41.42% on the deposit yet −5.72% vs holding), and only the holding comparison says whether providing liquidity beat doing nothing. Educational model only — a 50/50 constant-product position, fees counted in $ terms outside the pool with no compounding modelled; your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice. CLMM positions are range-based and differ.

25 · CLMM capital-efficiency calculator

Why concentrated liquidity exists at all: a position spread over every possible price — a constant-product pool, in CLMM terms the full range — keeps most of its capital parked at prices the market never visits. This compares the capital two positions need to carry the same model liquidity L at the current price: the ranged position holds Tool 9's amounts for that L, and a full-range position with the same L holds L/√P of A and L·√P of B. Capital efficiency is the ratio of the two values in B. For a geometrically centred range it collapses to 1 / (1 − 1/√m) — the 0.8–1.25 range at price 1 (m = 1.25) needs 9.47× less capital. The honest half the headline hides: that multiple is not free money — the ranged position earns fees only while the price stays inside the range, and its impermanent loss per unit of liquidity is amplified by the same concentration (Tool 12 measures it). The price must sit strictly inside the range; at or outside an edge the position is single-sided and out of the market, so no efficiency is quoted for it. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

26 · Pool depth planner

Tool 17 answers a trader's question — given a pool, how big a trade fits an impact cap? A pool creator asks the inverse: given the trade size you expect and the impact cap you want it to respect, how deep a pool must you fund? The same closed form, inverted: the input-side reserve that puts a trade exactly at its cap is amount × (1 − impact) × (1 − fee) ÷ (impact − fee), and the output-side reserve follows from the spot price you choose. Depth scales linearly — twice the expected trade needs twice the reserves — and a tighter cap needs a deeper pool. Two honest limits: a cap at or below the fee tier admits no positive trade at any depth (the fee alone counts toward impact in this measure), so that case is reported as impossible rather than an infinite reserve; and the depth is sized for one trade at the cap in an untouched pool — repeated same-direction trades each move the price further, and a deeper pool is not a safe pool: depth says nothing about the tokens, the price being right, or impermanent loss. Educational model only — the reserves are targets you would have to fund, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

27 · Post-move reserves calculator

Tool 2 prices a price move as a percentage, but percentages hide the mechanism: when the market price of token A moves, arbitrageurs trade against the pool until its price matches, and x×y=k forces what is left — at a price multiple r the pool ends up holding reserve A ÷ √r and reserve B × √r. The pool has sold the token that rose and bought the one that fell, which is exactly why it trails simply holding. This shows those rebalanced reserves, the change in each token, and the pool's value in B at the new price against holding the original reserves untouched — the ratio is Tool 2's own figure for the same move, so the two tools cannot disagree. One simplification, stated plainly: no fees are modelled, so k is held exactly constant; in a live pool the arbitrage trades pay the pool fee and k grows slightly, which softens the loss a little. The rebalanced reserves are not what you deposited — check them before assuming a withdrawal returns your original split. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

28 · Split-route swap planner

Tool 23 routes a trade through two pools in series; this is the parallel move aggregators also make — splitting one trade across two pools for the same pair. Each leg is smaller, so each moves its pool's price less, and the combined output beats sending the whole trade through either pool alone: two identical 1,000/1,000 pools split a 100-in trade evenly and return ≈95.24 against ≈90.91 from one pool. The planner searches the split using Tool 1's own model for every candidate leg, so each leg is exactly what Tool 1 quotes for it; deeper pools and lower-fee pools earn the larger share, and if one pool cannot take a trade at all, everything honestly routes through the other. Only two pools, same pair and direction — a real aggregator searches many venues, and network and transaction costs of an extra leg are not modelled. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

29 · CLMM net return calculator

Tool 24 settles a constant-product position against holding with the fees it actually earned — this is that settlement for a CLMM position, built entirely on Tool 12's own numbers. Enter the model liquidity Tool 8 reported, your range, the price you entered at, a price to check, and the fees you actually earned in token B: the calculator adds the fees to the position's value at the check price and gives the verdict against holding, in B and as a %. It also reports the return on the position's entry value — a different bottom line, reported alongside rather than instead: at the top edge of a 0.8–1.25 range the position is +5.90% on its entry value yet −5.87% vs holding with no fees, because holding kept all of the token that rose. At the entry price there is no hurdle, so coverage is honestly reported as nothing-to-cover rather than a percentage. Educational model only — fees counted in token B outside the position, no compounding or re-centring modelled; your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

30 · CLMM IL tolerance band

Tool 21 finds the price band a constant-product position's fees can defend; this is that band for a CLMM position, searched directly on Tool 12's own shortfall numbers, because a concentrated position's shortfall has no closed form — it changes character at the range edges. Enter the model liquidity Tool 8 reported, your range, the price you entered at (inside the range — a position entered outside it holds one token and has no two-sided band), and the fees you have actually earned in token B: the calculator reports the prices above and below entry at which those fees exactly stop covering the shortfall vs holding, and whether each edge sits inside your range or beyond it. The downside has a hard cap the upside does not: the most a fall can ever cost vs holding is the token B the position held at entry, so fees at or above that cap are reported as unconsumable by any fall rather than given a made-up edge. Educational model only — fees counted in token B outside the position, no fee growth or re-centring modelled; your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

31 · CLMM required-volume planner

Tool 15 asks how many days a given volume needs to cover the shortfall vs holding; this asks the planning question in reverse — how much volume the pool needs per day for your fees to cover that shortfall within a number of days you choose. Enter the position (the model liquidity Tool 8 reported, your range, entry and check prices) and the fee side (total active liquidity, fee tier, days allowed, time in range): the calculator takes Tool 12's own fee hurdle and runs Tool 13's fee formula backwards, so the volume it reports is exactly the volume Tools 13 and 15 would work forwards from. At the entry price there is no shortfall, so the required volume is honestly zero; with a real shortfall but a zero fee tier or 0% time in range, no volume earns a fee at all, so the answer is reported as not feasible rather than as a huge number. The volume is the whole pool's trading in token B per day, not your trades, and the fee rate is assumed to hold still — in a live pool it will not. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not a volume forecast, not financial advice.

32 · Constant-product required-volume planner

Tool 31 asks this planning question for a CLMM position; this is the same question for a constant-product (AMM v4 / CPMM / CP-Swap) position. Enter the price move you fear (as a multiple), your deposit, your liquidity and the pool's TVL — your share is the two divided — the fee tier, and the days you'd accept to recover: the calculator takes Tool 4's own break-even hurdle and runs Tool 3's fee formula backwards, so the volume it reports is exactly the volume Tools 3 and 4 would work forwards from. At no price move there is no shortfall, so the required volume is honestly zero; with a real shortfall but a zero fee tier, no volume earns a fee at all, so the answer is reported as not feasible rather than as a huge number. The volume is the whole pool's trading per day, in the deposit's terms, not your trades, and the fee rate is assumed to hold still — in a live pool it will not. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not a volume forecast, not financial advice.

33 · CLMM single-sided zap-in planner

Tool 19 zaps into a constant-product pool from one token; a CLMM entry from one token is different, because the deposit split is set by your range, not by a pool ratio. Enter the constant-product pool you'd swap in (its reserves and fee tier), the CLMM side (current price and the range you want), and the token A you hold: the planner solves for the one swap whose return in token B exactly funds the deposit of the A that remains, at the B-per-A ratio the range's own maths (Tool 8) requires at the current price — then runs the swap through Tool 1's model and settles the deposit through Tool 14's, so both legs are those tools' numbers. At the range's geometric centre the deposit is value-balanced; near the top edge almost everything must be swapped. If the price sits below your range, no swap is needed at all — the position is entirely token A; at or above the top, everything is swapped and the position is entirely token B. Because the swap leg rounds at 9 decimals, a dust of token A (about a billionth on a 100-token entry) can be left over, and it is reported rather than hidden. The swap pool and the CLMM position are modelled separately — in practice the swap routes wherever the price is best — and no tick-spacing snapping or price movement between the steps is modelled. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

34 · CLMM single-sided zap-out planner

Tool 33's exit mirror. Closing a CLMM position pays whatever it holds at the exit price (Tool 9's amounts for its liquidity and range) — inside the range that is both tokens, so leaving in token A alone means swapping the token-B leg away. Enter the position you are closing in full (its model liquidity L from Tool 8, its range, and the exit price) and the constant-product pool you'd swap in (its reserves and fee tier): the planner takes the position's holdings from Tool 9's own maths and runs the B leg through Tool 1's swap model, then prices the consolidation honestly the way Tool 20 does — against what the withdrawn pair would be worth in A if the B leg filled at the swap pool's spot price with no fee and no impact. At or below the lower edge the position is entirely token A already, so no swap is needed and the cost is zero; at or above the top it is entirely token B, so everything is swapped. The swap pool and the CLMM position are modelled separately — in practice the swap routes wherever the price is best. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

35 · CLMM token-B deposit planner

Tool 8's mirror. Tool 8 plans a CLMM deposit from a token-A amount you choose; if the token you hold first is token B — a stablecoin side, say — the question runs the other way: given a current price, a chosen price range and a token-B deposit, the matching token-A amount the range's position maths requires (the split is set by where the price sits in the range), the model's liquidity, and the range's tick indices. Inside the range a falling price means the B you deposit is the smaller side, so the matching A grows; at or above the top of the range the position is entirely token B, so no token A is required at all; at or below the bottom it is entirely token A, so a token-B deposit cannot fund it and that combination is rejected rather than given a made-up match — the exact mirror of Tool 8 rejecting a token-A deposit at or above the top. Tick indices are modelled with floating-point logs and are not snapped to a pool's tick spacing; no fees are modelled. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

36 · CLMM re-centre / rebalance planner

The follow-up to tools 9 and 22. The price has moved and your position's token mix has drifted with it; re-centring means closing the old position and opening a new range centred on the price you have now, at a width you choose (built multiplicatively symmetric, tool 22's rule — so the room differs by side: ±25% of width is +25% up but only −20% down). Given your position's model liquidity (tool 8 reports it), its old range, the current price and the new width, this planner reads what the position holds now (tool 9's maths), sizes the new liquidity so the re-centred position is worth exactly the same at the current price — a rebalance adds and removes nothing — and states the one swap that gets you there. Because the new range is centred, its target holdings are always split 50/50 by value at the current price; if the price has left your old range entirely, exactly half the position's value swaps into the other token, and if your old range already is the centred range, no swap is needed at all. The swap is priced at the current spot price, stated plainly: a real swap also pays its fee and price impact (tools 1, 33 and 34 model that cost), so treat the swap size as the plan, not the fill. No fees earned are added and ticks are not snapped to a pool's tick spacing. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

37 · CLMM withdrawal planner

Tool 7's exit for a concentrated position. Tool 7 plans a withdrawal from a constant-product pool, and tool 34 closes a CLMM position only in full and only into one token; this plans the middle case — withdraw a percentage of a CLMM position's liquidity and receive both tokens, in the mix the position holds at the price you withdraw at. A position's holdings are proportional to its liquidity, so withdrawing p% of the liquidity pays exactly p% of what the position holds at that price (tool 9's amounts), and what stays behind is the same range at (100 − p)% of the liquidity — its mix does not change, only its size. At 100% this is the full close: you receive everything the position holds and nothing remains. If the price has left the range, the position is a single token, so the withdrawal is that token alone — exiting an out-of-range position does not bring the other token back. No fees earned are added to the payout (a real CLMM withdrawal collects accrued fees separately) and no withdrawal fee is modelled. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

38 · Constant-product wallet-balance deposit planner

Tool 14's question for an ordinary constant-product pool (AMM v4 / CPMM / CP-Swap). Tool 5 asks how much token B a chosen token-A deposit needs — this asks the wallet's question instead: you hold a fixed amount of both tokens, and the deposit must land in the pool's existing ratio, so what is the biggest deposit those balances can fund, and what is left over? Whichever side is scarcer in ratio terms caps the deposit and is used in full; the other side is partly left unused. If your balances are exactly in the pool's ratio, both are used in full. On the token-B-capped side the model's 9-decimal flooring can leave a few smallest units of token B behind as dust. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

39 · Two-hop exact-out swap model

Tool 23 routes a fixed amount in through two pools, and tool 6 prices a fixed amount out of one pool — this composes the two: you need an exact amount of token C, so how much token A must you pay through an A → M pool and then an M → C pool? The model works backwards with tool 6's own maths on each leg: hop 2's exact-out on your target gives the intermediate token M hop 2 must receive, and hop 1's exact-out on that amount gives the token A to pay. Each hop rounds its required input up the way tool 6 does, so paying the reported amount forward returns at least the target in this model. A target a pool cannot pay — at or above pool 2's token-C reserve, or needing at least pool 1's whole token-M reserve — is rejected, not priced. Educational model only — both pools are constant-product here, no routing search is done, and these are the two pools you typed, with your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.

40 · Constant-product break-even days calculator

Tool 15 answers "how long until fees cover the shortfall?" for a CLMM position; this is the same question for a constant-product (AMM v4 / CPMM / CP-Swap) position. Tool 4 gives the shortfall a price move opens against holding, and tool 3 gives the fees a day of pool volume pays you at your share — until now you had to run the second and type its answer into the first by hand. This joins them: enter the price move you fear (as a multiple), your deposit, the pool's volume per day, the fee tier, and your liquidity against the pool's TVL, and it divides tool 4's own hurdle by tool 3's own daily fees. At no price move there is no shortfall, so the answer is honestly 0 days even at zero volume; with a real shortfall but no volume, or a zero fee tier, the position never breaks even and that is what it says — not a huge number. The day count assumes volume, tier, share and price all hold still, which in a live pool they will not. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not a forecast, not financial advice.

41 · Split-route exact-out swap model

Tool 28 splits a fixed amount in across two pools for the same pair; tool 6 prices a fixed amount out of one pool. This composes them: enter the exact amount out you need and two pools for the same pair, and it splits the target across them so the total amount in is smallest — each leg priced by tool 6's own exact-out maths, so the legs can never drift from that tool. Two identical 1,000/1,000 pools at 25 bps split a target of exactly 100 about evenly and charge ≈105.53 in total, against ≈111.39 from either pool alone. Deeper pools and lower-fee pools take the larger share of the target, and a pool that cannot supply any of it is routed around entirely. One honest case the single-pool tool cannot express: a target at or above either pool's whole reserve out has no single-pool price at all, yet the two pools combined can still supply it — then splitting is not a saving, it is the only route, and the tool says so rather than inventing a comparison. A target at or above the two reserves out combined can never be paid out, so it is rejected, not priced. Only two pools, same pair and direction — a real aggregator searches many venues, and the extra leg's network and transaction costs are not modelled. Educational model only — your inputs, not live pool state; not a live quote, not financial advice.
